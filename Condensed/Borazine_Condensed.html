Borazine · history

Discovery and name

  • Stock's vacuum line made the work possible, but early yields were only "a few millilitres of vapour".
  • The name: Wiberg's "Borazol" became borazole; an ACS committee recommended borazine, which Sheldon and Smith in 1960 still declined to use.

Same ring, more early chemistry

As early as 1889 Rideal made "BClNPh" from BCl3 and aniline. Jones and Kinney (1939) showed it was a trimer, B-trichloro-N-triphenylborazine, and related it to borazole.

Sheldon & Smith (1960), pp. 200–203, refs. 1–5, 25–26: A. Stock, E. Pohland, Ber. 1926, 59, 2215; A. Stock, R. Wierl, Z. anorg. Chem. 1931, 203, 228; E. Wiberg, A. Bolz, Ber. 1940, 73, 209.
A short history, tying back to Stock. Stock and Pohland obtained borazine by heating the diborane–ammonia adduct.

The estimated single-bond length depends on the radii used: Sheldon and Smith estimate 1.45 Å, Li et al. 1.57 Å. With the larger value, borazine's 1.44 Å implies substantial π bonding; with the smaller, very little. The bond-order argument is therefore only qualitative. Sheldon and Smith estimate about 20 % double-bond character in B-trichloroborazine.
B–N and C–C

Isoelectronic, but polar

  • B (3 e) + N (5 e) = C (4 e) + C (4 e): a B–N unit is isoelectronic with C–C.
  • But N is much more electronegative than B (Pauling 3.04 vs 2.04). The σ bond is polarised Bδ+–Nδ−.
  • N's lone pair can be donated into B's empty p orbital: a dative π bond N→B, which pushes electron density the other way.
  • Result: "olefin-like" aminoboranes R2B=NR2 are only qualitatively like alkenes. They are not rigidly planar (B–N rotation barrier ≈ 20 kcal mol⁻¹, far below C=C) and often dimerise.
Muetterties (1967), ch. 1, pp. 19–20; Wade (1971), §4-4, p. 86; electronegativities: Pauling scale.
The whole deck is about this tension: same electron count as carbon, very different polarity. The π donation partly offsets the σ polarity, but not completely.
Borazine · bonding

The π system: shared, but unevenly

  • Both rings have six π electrons in three bonding π MOs.
  • In benzene the six p orbitals are identical: the MOs are spread evenly.
  • In borazine the N 2p orbitals lie lower in energy than the B 2p. The filled π MOs are mainly on N; the empty ones mainly on B.
  • So the π electrons are partly localised as N lone pairs: delocalisation is real but weaker than in benzene.
Muetterties (1967), p. 20; Sheldon & Smith (1960), pp. 215–217 (Roothaan & Mulliken MO treatment); Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3. Energy diagram schematic.
This is the key bonding idea: an electronegativity difference splits the degenerate p orbital set and polarises the π MOs. Modern calculations generally describe borazine as weakly aromatic, far less than benzene.
Borazine · structures

Writing borazine: three ways

σ only: N keeps its lone pairs; B has empty p orbitals. No formal charges.

Kekulé-type: three dative N=B π bonds, with formal N+ and B−. Two such forms.

Circle: delocalised π, like benzene. Convenient, but hides the polarity.

Sheldon & Smith (1960), p. 200 (Kekulé structures accepted); Muetterties (1967), p. 20.
The formal charges in the Kekulé form (B−, N+) are opposite to the actual partial charges (B δ+, N δ−). This is a frequent source of confusion: formal charges are bookkeeping, not real charge distribution.
Borazine · bonding

How aromatic is borazine?

Benzene-like

  • Planar ring; six equal B–N bonds (1.44 Å), shorter than a B–N single bond.
  • Six π electrons in three bonding π MOs.
  • UV bands and ring vibrations close to benzene's.

Not benzene-like

  • π electrons concentrated on N: polar Bδ+–Nδ− ring.
  • Adds HX, H2O, MeOH across the ring; benzene does not.
  • Decomposes slowly as a liquid at room temperature.

Verdict

Borazine has a delocalised but uneven π system: weakly aromatic, far less than benzene.

Sheldon & Smith (1960), pp. 210, 215–217; Muetterties (1967), p. 20; Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3.

The common pattern

A B–H or B–Cl bond meets an N–H bond; H2 or HCl is lost and the ring closes at about 200 °C.

Sheldon & Smith (1960), pp. 201–204 (Stock & Pohland; Schaeffer, Schaeffer & Schlesinger 1951; Brown & Laubengayer 1955; Schaeffer et al. 1954). *Modern route from memory (Wideman and Sneddon, 1995; Greenwood & Earnshaw): check before quoting; equation balanced here.
The first equation is the overall stoichiometry of Stock's route, written for the parent compound. Free NH3 with BCl3 gives only traces of borazine; NH4Cl moderates the reaction.
Making borazines · mechanism

From ammonia borane to boron nitride, by losing H2

  • Wiberg's scheme: adduct → aminoborane → iminoborane → trimer. The free iminoborane HB≡NH is not isolable; Sheldon and Smith suggest cyclisation occurs at an earlier stage, through the saturated trimer.
  • The same sequence explains why NH3 + B2H6 gives borazine at about 200 °C and boron nitride at about 400 °C.
Sheldon & Smith (1960), pp. 207–209 (Wiberg's condensation chain); Wade (1971), §4-4, Fig. 4-11.
This is also the chemistry of ammonia borane as a hydrogen store: each step releases H2, and the end product is boron nitride. Regenerating ammonia borane from the spent material is the hard part.

Reactions

Addition, not substitution

  • HCl, HBr, H2O, MeOH and MeI add three molecules across the ring, even with excess borazine: B3N3H6·3HX.
  • Regiochemistry follows polarity: the nucleophile goes to B (δ+), H to N (δ−).
  • On heating it loses 3 H2 to give the B-substituted borazine, e.g. B3Cl3N3H3: overall substitution by addition–elimination.

Why all three?

Adding the first HX destroys much of the ring's π stabilisation; adding the next two then costs little, so 1 : 1 and 1 : 2 adducts are rare.

Sheldon & Smith (1960), pp. 206, 210 (adducts B3H3N3H3·3AD; structure VII).
Benzene does not add HCl at all under these conditions. The contrast is the clearest evidence that borazine's aromatic stabilisation is much smaller than benzene's.

B- and N-substituted borazines

Naming as in Sheldon & Smith (1960), p. 203: "B-trichloro" means one Cl on each of the three B; "N-trimethyl" one Me on each N. Drawings schematic (dashed circle: delocalised π).

Two mechanisms

HX, H2O, MeOH: all three add at once, then H2 is lost. RMgX, BR3, BX3: one B–H at a time, giving mono-, di- and tri-substituted products.

Derivatives · chemical properties

How substituents change the chemistry

  • B–Cl compounds are reactive: moist air hydrolyses them, and B3Cl3N3H3 slowly loses HCl even at room temperature.
  • Alkyl groups protect the ring: N-alkyl blocks condensation through N–H, so alkyl derivatives are thermally more stable than borazine.
  • Hydrolysis of B-methyl compounds keeps the B–C bonds: B3Me3N3Me3 + H2O → B3Me3O3 (a boroxine).
  • B-amino derivatives lose amine above 300 °C and cross-link to polymers stable to 600 °C.
  • Substitution stays at B: no N-chloro or N-nitro borazine was known in 1960.

π complexes

B3Cl3N3(C6H4Me)3 crystallises with one benzene per borazine; π-complexes with arenes were expected but little studied by 1960.

Sheldon & Smith (1960), pp. 205, 207–211.
The boroxine B3Me3O3 is the oxygen analogue of the borazine ring: B3O3 instead of B3N3.

Summary

Key points

  • Borazine is a planar D3h ring with equal B–N bonds (1.44 Å), isoelectronic with benzene.
  • Its π electrons lie mainly on N: the ring is polar and only weakly aromatic.
  • Made by condensing B–H or B–Cl with N–H (B2H6 + NH3; LiBH4 + NH4Cl; BCl3 + NH4Cl, then LiBH4).
  • Physically benzene-like; chemically it adds HX and H2O across the ring, nucleophile to B.
  • Derivatives: N-substituents from the amine, B-substituents from the boron reagent or by replacing B–Cl.
Sheldon & Smith (1960); Muetterties (1967), ch. 1.

Questions (press → to reveal each answer)

1. Why does HCl add to borazine but not to benzene?
The ring is polar (Bδ+, Nδ−) and its π stabilisation is small: Cl⁻ goes to B, H⁺ to N.
2. How would you make B-trimethyl-N-trimethylborazine?
BCl3 + MeNH3Cl → B3Cl3N3Me3; then 3 MeMgBr.
3. Why does Kekulé-type borazine carry N⁺ and B⁻ formal charges?
The N=B π bonds are dative from N; formal charges are bookkeeping, opposite to the real partial charges.
4. Which is the electrophilic site in borazine?
Boron: all substitutions and additions bring the nucleophile to B.
