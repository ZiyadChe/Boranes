Examples · the closo family

The BnHn²⁻ family, n = 6 to 12

Cages and symmetries as in Wade (1971), Fig. 3-16, p. 51; Hawthorne (1967), §5.6A. Models are idealised deltahedra with one terminal H on each B; front edges drawn heavier.
Every member is a deltahedron (all faces triangles), and every boron carries exactly one terminal H. There are no B–H–B bridges.

Structure · common features

What the closo anions have in common

B12H12²⁻ · Ih
drag to rotate
  • Closed deltahedra: every face is a triangle; no open face.
  • Each B has one terminal H, pointing outwards along the radius. There are no B–H–B bridges and no BH2 groups.
  • Borons are 4-, 5- or 6-connected; the cages are as nearly spherical as the triangles allow.
  • Neutral analogues: the carboranes C2Bn−2Hn (CH in place of BH⁻).
Wade (1971), §3-5, pp. 50–56; Hawthorne (1967), §5.6A–B. Models idealised.
Compare with the open boranes (B5H9, B10H14), which have bridges and BH2 groups on their open faces.

Structure · ¹¹B NMR

¹¹B NMR shows the symmetry

  • B12H12²⁻: one doublet. All twelve B are equivalent (Ih); each couples to its one H.
  • B10H10²⁻: two doublets, 1 : 4: two apical B at low field, eight belt B.
  • The B10H10²⁻ structure was deduced from this spectrum, then confirmed by X-ray of Cu2B10H10.
Hawthorne (1967), §5.6A.1–2, pp. 287, 289. Spectra schematic.
Substitution lowers the symmetry, so NMR also shows where a substituent sits.

Preparation · what happens to the cage

Closing an open cage, and growing a new one

Closing B10: L leaves B6 or B9; Et3N removes H⁺ (B10H11⁻), then a second H⁺ (B10H10²⁻).

Growing B12: B2H6 + BH4⁻ → B3H8⁻ → … → B11H14⁻ → B12H12²⁻, adding BH3 and losing H2.

Driving force

Each step ends nearer a closed deltahedron with no bridges: the most stable arrangement for n + 1 pairs.

Hawthorne (1967), §5.6A.1–2, pp. 287–289, Fig. 5.25 (reproduced); Mingos (2021), pp. 17–18.
Count: B10H12L2 has 13 skeletal pairs (arachno); B10H10 2− has 11 (closo). The two L leave with their electron pairs.

  • Contrast B5H9 (ignites in air) and B2H6 (hydrolysed at once).
  • No open face, no bridges, and a closed shell of n + 1 pairs: no easy path for attack.

"Aromatic" boron hydrides

Hawthorne grouped the closo anions and carboranes as aromatic: stabilised by delocalisation, in three dimensions.

Hawthorne (1967), §5.6 intro and §5.6A.3a, pp. 286, 289–290.
Large doses of Na2B10H10 were given to humans without serious effect, which mattered for later medical uses.

  • B10H10²⁻ is much more reactive than B12H12²⁻. H/D exchange rates: B12 1; B10 belt 68; B10 apex 330.
  • The apical B of B10H10²⁻ carry the most negative charge, so many electrophiles attack there first.
  • B12Cl12²⁻ is even more inert than B12H12²⁻.
Hawthorne (1967), §5.6A.3b–c, pp. 290–297; charges: R. Hoffmann, W. N. Lipscomb, J. Chem. Phys. 1962, 36, 2179; Das, §10.13.2(d).
Not every reagent goes apical: amination and benzoylation go to the belt. Hawthorne suggested more than one mechanism of electrophilic substitution.
Properties · neutral derivatives

Replacing H⁻ by a neutral donor removes the charge

B10H10²⁻ → (HNO2, then NaBH4) → 1,10-B10H8(N2)2

  • N2, CO, NR3 or SMe2 replace H⁻; each cancels one negative charge.
  • N2 is displaced by amines and nitriles; 1,10-B10H8(CO)2 reacts like an acyl compound.

Same electron count

A neutral donor L gives the exo B–L bond the same two electrons as H⁻, so the cage keeps n + 1 skeletal pairs and stays closo.

Hawthorne (1967), §5.6A.3b, pp. 294–295 (Knoth and co-workers).
The carboranes are the built-in version: CH replaces BH−.
Properties · reactions of the cage

Cages that rearrange and cages that join

Rearrangement

  • 1,10-, 2,7- and 1,6-B10H8(NMe3)2 interconvert at 300–325 °C; the cage itself reorganises.
  • B12H10(NMe3)2: no clear change up to 500 °C.

Oxidative coupling

2 B10H10²⁻ + 4 Fe³⁺ → B20H18²⁻ + 2 H⁺ + 4 Fe²⁺

  • Two B10 cages joined by three-centre bonds.
  • B12H12²⁻ does not couple.
Hawthorne (1967), §5.6A.3d–e, pp. 297–300, Figs 5.27–5.28.
Na/NH3 reduction of B20H18 2− gives B20H18 4−: two B10H9 units joined by a two-centre B–B bond.
Did you know?

Boron cages against cancer

  • Boron neutron capture therapy: 10B captures a slow neutron and splits into an α particle and 7Li, which travel about one cell diameter.
  • A cluster carries many B atoms per molecule; Na2B12H11SH ("BSH") has been used clinically. The low toxicity of the closo anions made this possible.

Part D.
Bonding · the problem

Localised bonds cannot explain the 2− charge

  • Lipscomb's equations give acceptable 2c/3c schemes for neutral BnHn and for BnHn⁴⁻ as easily as for the 2− ions.
  • So the valence-bond picture cannot say why only the 2− ions exist.
  • For large cages it also needs many resonance forms.
  • A molecular orbital treatment using the full cage symmetry gives the answer: n + 1 bonding MOs.
Wade (1971), §3-5, pp. 52–53, Fig. 3-17 (reproduced), Table 3-3.
Wade: "A much clearer indication of the bonding electron requirements of a particular cage is given by molecular orbital approaches which take account of the cage symmetries."
Bonding · orbitals per vertex

Each B–H vertex gives one radial and two tangential orbitals

One sp hybrid forms the exo B–H bond; the other points to the centre: the radial orbital.

Two p orbitals lie along the cage surface: the tangential orbitals.

An n-vertex cage: 3n skeletal orbitals (n radial + 2n tangential).

Wade (1971), pp. 54–57; Das, §10.12.7; Mingos (2021), p. 27. Schematic drawings.
Boron has four valence orbitals: one for the exo B–H bond, three for the cage. Each BH unit brings two electrons to the cage.

Electrons: n BH × 2 + 2 (charge) = 2n + 2 = n + 1 pairs.

B6H6²⁻: 18 orbitals → 7 bonding, 14 electrons.

B12H12²⁻: 36 orbitals → 13 bonding, 26 electrons.

Wade (1971), pp. 54–57, Fig. 3-19 (reproduced); Mingos (2021), p. 27 ("the 2n linear combinations of tangential p orbitals result in n bonding and n antibonding MOs").
Wade also notes that substituents affect the radial set inductively and the tangential set mesomerically.

Bonding · MO diagram

18 skeletal orbitals → 7 bonding MOs in B6H6²⁻

Das, §10.12.7, Fig. 10.12.7.1; H. C. Longuet-Higgins, M. de V. Roberts, Proc. R. Soc. Lond. A 1955, 230, 1; Hoffmann & Lipscomb (1962). Energies not to scale.
Radial set: a1g + t1u + eg. Tangential set: t1u + t2g + t2u + t1g. The two t1u sets mix. Filled: a1g, t1u, t2g = 7 pairs. Neutral B6H6 would have only 6 pairs: the triply degenerate t2g set would be part-filled, giving two unpaired electrons (Wade, p. 55).

Did you know?

B12H12²⁻ was predicted before it was made

  • Longuet-Higgins and Roberts (1954–55) calculated the MOs of the B6 and B12 cages found in metal borides.
  • A closed shell needed a 2− charge: they predicted B6H6²⁻ and B12H12²⁻.
  • B12H12²⁻ salts were isolated in 1960; B6H6²⁻ in 1964.

Summary

Key points

  • BnHn²⁻ (n = 6–12) are closed deltahedra, one terminal H per B, no bridges.
  • Made by base closure of B10H14 (B10H10²⁻) or by building up from B2H6 and BH4⁻ (B12H12²⁻).
  • Very stable to heat, acid and base; react by substitution; low toxicity.
  • Each BH gives 1 radial + 2 tangential orbitals: 3n orbitals give n + 1 bonding MOs, filled by 2n + 2 electrons.
Wade (1971), ch. 3; Hawthorne (1967), §5.6A; Das, §10.12.7.
Ask students to derive n + 1 for B10H10 2−.
Check your understanding

Questions (press → to reveal each answer)

1. How many skeletal pairs does B10H10²⁻ have, and which shape?
10 × 2 + 2 = 22 e = 11 pairs = n + 1: closo, bicapped square antiprism.
2. Why does neutral B6H6 not exist?
Only 6 pairs for 7 bonding MOs: the t2g set is part-filled, giving a diradical.
3. Which MO of B6H6²⁻ uses only radial orbitals?
a1g: all six inward sp lobes in phase.
4. Why is B10H10²⁻ attacked at the apex first?
The apical B carry the most negative charge.
