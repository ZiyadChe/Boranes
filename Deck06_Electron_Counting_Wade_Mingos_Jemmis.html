Learning outcomes

By the end of this deck you should be able to

  • Count skeletal electron pairs (SEP) for boranes, carboranes, heteroboranes and metal fragments.
  • Use Wade's rules (n + 1, n + 2, n + 3, n + 4) to predict closo, nido, arachno or hypho shapes.
  • Explain from the MO picture why a closo deltahedron needs exactly n + 1 pairs.
  • Apply isolobal fragments and Mingos's total electron counts (4n + 2, 14n + 2) and the capping principle.
  • Use Jemmis's mno rule for condensed polyhedra and metallocenes, and recognise where counting rules fail.

Part A: the historical setting.
The puzzle around 1970 · history

From prediction to rule, 1954–2001

Mingos (2021), §§5–9 and refs. 65–66, 82–91, 106–117, 163–166, 178, 185, 262–263.
Walk through the timeline. Note the decade-long gap between the discovery of the closo anions (1959–60) and the realisation, in 1971, that the open neutral boranes are fragments of the same deltahedra. Mingos calls this "a bit of a mystery".
Did you know?

B12H12²⁻ was predicted before it was made

  • In 1954–55 Longuet-Higgins and Roberts calculated the MOs of the B6 octahedron and the B12 icosahedron found in metal borides.
  • A closed shell needed a 2− charge: they predicted stable B6H6²⁻ and B12H12²⁻.
  • Salts of B12H12²⁻ were isolated in 1960, stable to air, boiling water and strong acid. Mingos calls it "a triumph of semi-quantitative molecular orbital calculations in the pre-computer days".

A structural pattern, and within months an electronic reason for it.

Part B: Wade's rules.
Williams and Wade, 1971

One picture: shapes arranged by skeletal pairs

  • Bottom row: closo BnHn²⁻, n = 4–11.
  • Diagonal arrows: remove one vertex (nido), then another (arachno). The number of skeletal pairs stays the same along each arrow.
  • B6H6²⁻ → B5H9 → B4H10: 7 pairs each. B11H11²⁻ → B10H14 → B9H15: 12 pairs each.
  • Williams supplied the pattern; Wade, within six months, the electron count.
Mingos (2021), §7–8, Fig. 16; R. W. Rudolph, Acc. Chem. Res. 1976, 9, 446; Williams (1971).
This diagram, Rudolph's matrix, is reproduced in most textbooks. Use it as the anchor for the whole deck.
Williams and Wade, 1971

Wade's insight: count pairs, not bonds

  • Lipscomb counted localised bonds (Deck 4). Wade counted only the total number of electron pairs available for the cage skeleton.
  • Closo BnHn²⁻ ions all have n + 1 skeletal pairs. A species with n atoms and n + 2 pairs has "one pair too many for the closed cage … but just the right number for a cage with (n + 1) corners".
  • He extended it at once to metal carbonyl clusters: B6H6²⁻ and [Ru6(CO)18]²⁻ are both octahedral with 7 pairs.
"Boranes, apparent rule-breakers, had become pattern-makers."K. Wade, Nature Chem. 2009, 1, 92
Wade, Chem. Commun. 1971, 792; Wade, Electron Deficient Compounds (1971), pp. 152–154, as quoted in Mingos (2021), pp. 26–29.
Wade's communication contained no figures. Mingos considers its most original part the link to transition-metal carbonyl clusters. Wade was a postdoc with Emeléus at Cambridge in the late 1950s working on boranes as rocket fuels.

General rule for any vertex

main group: v + x − 2
transition metal: v + x − 12

v = valence electrons of the atom; x = electrons from its exo ligands. The 2 or 12 electrons stay in exo bonds and non-skeletal orbitals; the vertex gives three orbitals to the skeleton.

Then SEP = (total skeletal electrons) ÷ 2.

Mingos (2021), Table 2 and p. 34; Das, §10.12.8–10.12.9; Wade, Adv. Inorg. Chem. Radiochem. 1976, 18, 1.
The main-group formula: v + x − 2. For B–H, 3 + 1 − 2 = 2. For the metal formula, 12 electrons fill the six non-skeletal orbitals of a nine-orbital metal: Fe(CO)3 gives 8 + 6 − 12 = 2, the same as BH.

Williams and Wade, 1971 · the reason

Why n + 1? Start with three orbitals per vertex

After the exo B–H bond, each boron keeps one sp hybrid pointing to the cage centre: the radial orbital.

It also keeps two p orbitals lying along the cage surface: the tangential orbitals.

An n-vertex cage therefore has 3n skeletal orbitals: n radial + 2n tangential. For B6H6²⁻, 6 + 12 = 18.

Wade, Electron Deficient Compounds (1971), ch. 3; Das, §10.12.7 ("radial" and "tangential" orbitals); Mingos (2021), p. 27. Schematic drawings.
Each BH vertex: B has four valence orbitals; one sp hybrid makes the exo B–H bond, leaving one sp (radial, pointing in) and two p (tangential). Two electrons remain for the skeleton, which is why BH counts as 2.
Williams and Wade, 1971 · the reason

The 18 skeletal orbitals of B6H6²⁻ give 7 bonding MOs

Das, §10.12.7, Fig. 10.12.7.1 (a1g; t2g from in-plane σ-type overlap of tangential p; t1u from π-type tangential overlap with radial); Longuet-Higgins & Roberts (1955); Hoffmann & Lipscomb, J. Chem. Phys. 1962, 36, 2179. Orbital pictures are schematic views of one equatorial B4 ring; energies not to scale.
Walk up the diagram. a1g: six radial lobes all pointing in, same phase. t1u: one of three components shown; radial lobes of a trans pair with opposite phases plus parallel tangential p orbitals, together like a large p orbital. t2g: one of three components; tangential p orbitals of an equatorial ring arranged head-to-tail, + meeting +. The LUMO t2u has the same ring but adjacent lobes of opposite phase. The 11 empty MOs are t2u, t1g, eg and the antibonding t1u.
Williams and Wade, 1971 · confirmation

Adding electron pairs opens the cage

  • Rudolph and Pretzer (1972) kept n = 5 fixed and added electron pairs, following orbital energies along the distortion (Walsh diagrams, extended Hückel).
  • Each extra pair favours a more open fragment of a larger deltahedron: closo → nido → arachno.
  • They likened the closo shells to three-dimensional aromaticity, the counterpart of Hückel's 4p + 2 rule.
  • Caution: the TBP was only ≈ 1.5 eV below the square pyramid for B5H5²⁻; energy surfaces can be soft.
R. W. Rudolph, W. R. Pretzer, Inorg. Chem. 1972, 11, 1974; Mingos (2021), §9.1, pp. 29–32.
This is the "vertical" reading of the matrix: same n, more electrons. Deck 5 showed the chemistry of it: adding 2 e− or 2 L to B10H14 opens it from nido to arachno.
Williams and Wade, 1971 · analogy

An analogy with VSEPR

  • VSEPR: SF6, BrF5 and XeF4 all have 6 electron pairs and an octahedral arrangement; lone pairs replace F atoms.
  • Wade: B6H6²⁻, B5H9 and B4H10 all have 7 skeletal pairs and an octahedral parent; vertices are removed (formally as BH²⁺, with H⁺ added to keep the count).

One difference

VSEPR removes trans positions (square-planar XeF4); Wade removes adjacent vertices (butterfly B4H10). Bonding in VSEPR is radial; in clusters it is largely tangential.

Mingos (2021), pp. 28–29, Fig. 17.
Students know VSEPR well, so this is a good memory aid. Formally: B6H6 2− minus BH 2+ = B5H5 4−; add 4 H+ = B5H9.

Part C: polyhedral skeletal electron pair theory (PSEPT).
Mingos · isolobal fragments

B–H and Fe(CO)3: different atoms, same frontier orbitals

  • Both fragments point three orbitals at the cage and supply 2 electrons: they are isolobal.
  • Fe(CO)3: 8 + 3 × 2 − 12 = 2. Co(CO)3: 9 + 6 − 12 = 3, like C–H.
  • Interchangeable sets: BH, Fe(CO)3, Ni(CO)2 · CH, P, Co(CO)3.
  • Hawthorne had already shown that BH, CH⁺, Fe(η-C5H5) and Co(C2B9H11) interchange in metallacarboranes (Deck 8).
D. M. P. Mingos, Nature Phys. Sci. 1972, 236, 99; Mingos (2021), pp. 22, 33–38; isolobal analogy: R. Hoffmann, Nobel lecture, Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 1982, 21, 711.
The isolobal idea, made general by Hoffmann, is the bridge between boranes and organometallic clusters. The drawing is a cartoon of the three frontier orbitals, not a computed picture.

Bridging ligands count too

μ-H, μ-Me, μ-Ph give 1; μ-CO 2; μ-PR2, μ-SR 3. The count then does not depend on whether a CO is terminal or bridging, which was Mingos's practical advance for carbonyl clusters.

Why the 10n difference?

A metal has nine valence orbitals, a main-group atom four: each metal vertex needs 10 more electrons, the difference between the 18-electron and octet rules.

Mingos (2021), Table 4 and pp. 50–52.
Check one: [Os6(CO)18]2−: 6 × 8 + 18 × 2 + 2 = 86 = 14 × 6 + 2. Co4(CO)12: 4 × 9 + 24 = 60 = 15 × 4, a tetrahedron counted as a 3-connected polyhedron.
Mingos · capping

The capping principle: more vertices, no more pairs

  • A cap over a triangular face adds no new bonding skeletal MO: its three orbitals all go into antibonding combinations.
  • So a capped cluster has the same number of skeletal pairs as its parent; each cap adds 12 to the total valence count of a metal cluster.
  • Os6(CO)18: 84 electrons, two fewer than closo-octahedral [Os6(CO)18]²⁻. Observed: a bicapped tetrahedron (60 + 2 × 12).
  • Capping is the reverse of the debor principle: closo = "capped nido", nido = "capped arachno".
R. Mason, K. M. Thomas, D. M. P. Mingos, J. Am. Chem. Soc. 1973, 95, 3802; D. M. P. Mingos, M. I. Forsyth, J. Chem. Soc., Dalton Trans. 1977, 611; Mingos (2021), §10.1.
Formally a BH cap must carry a 2+ charge to leave the bonding-electron count unchanged, just as a bridging H carries 1+. Mingos tells how Mason solved the Os6(CO)18 structure but could not interpret it until the capping idea was suggested.
Part D

Jemmis: condensed polyhedra

When cages share vertices, edges or faces, Wade's rule needs extra terms.

D
Part D: the mno rule.
Jemmis · the problem

Where Wade's rule stops

  • Wade's n + 1 applies to one polyhedron.
  • B20H16: two icosahedra sharing four borons. Count: 16 BH × 2 + 4 B × 3 = 44 electrons = 22 pairs for n = 20. Wade's n + 1 = 21 is wrong.
  • Ferrocene: two pentagonal pyramids sharing the Fe vertex. 10 CH × 3 + Fe 2 = 32 = 16 pairs, n = 11.
  • The extra pairs depend on how many polyhedra there are and how they are joined.
E. D. Jemmis, M. M. Balakrishnarajan, P. D. Pancharatna, J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2001, 123, 4313; Chem. Rev. 2002, 102, 93; Das, §10.12.10.
The ferrocene count treats Fe as a shared vertex contributing 8 − 6 = 2 electrons, as in Jemmis's scheme. The B20H16 count is a good check students can do themselves.
Jemmis · the rule

The mno rule

SEP = m + n + o + p

m

number of polyhedra

n

number of vertices

o

number of single-vertex junctions between polyhedra

p

number of missing vertices (0 closo, 1 nido, 2 arachno per cage)

For a single cage, m = 1 and o = 0: SEP = n + 1 + p, which is Wade's rule. Edge and face sharing need no extra term.

Jemmis, Balakrishnarajan, Pancharatna, J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2001, 123, 4313; Das, §10.12.10.
Rationale in one line: each polyhedron contributes its own "+1" (the radial a1g-type orbital), and a single shared vertex cannot supply the orbitals needed by both cages, adding one more pair.

Part E

Limits of counting

Electron counts are guides, not laws.

E
Part E.
Limits

When counting rules fail

  • Same shape, different count. B8Cl8 and B9Cl9 (neutral, n pairs) are isostructural with B8H8²⁻ and B9H9²⁻ (n + 1). "Hypercloso" B12(OCH2Ph)12 keeps an icosahedron with 12 pairs.
  • Same count, different shape. [Os6(CO)18]²⁻ is a closo octahedron; isoelectronic Os6H2(CO)18 is a capped nido structure.
  • Exceptions. B8H12 is closer to arachno-B9H15 than to nido-B9.
  • Wrong polyhedron. MO calculations favoured a square antiprism for B8H8²⁻; the dodecahedron is observed.
"There are no such things as electron-deficient compounds, only theory-deficient chemists."R. E. Rundle, quoted by Wade (1971)

Mingos's verdict

Electron counting is an aide-mémoire. When it fails, the symmetries of the frontier orbitals explain why. It is "unreasonable to suggest that a single rule persists for all polyhedral molecules".

Mingos (2021), pp. 19–24, 44–45, 49, 52–55; hypercloso: T. Peymann, C. B. Knobler, S. I. Khan, M. F. Hawthorne, Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 2001, 40, 1664.
Mingos remarks that if benzene had come with a C6Cl6 2− analogue, Hückel might never have formulated aromaticity. Counting rules are powerful but not exceptionless.
Summary

Key points

  • Skeletal electrons: BH 2, CH 3, extra H 1, charge; any vertex gives v + x − 2 (main group) or v + x − 12 (metal).
  • Wade: n + 1 closo, n + 2 nido, n + 3 arachno, n + 4 hypho; Williams: remove the most connected vertex, then an adjacent one.
  • Reason: one radial bonding MO plus n tangential bonding MOs = n + 1.
  • Mingos: isolobal fragments (BH ≈ Fe(CO)3), totals 4n + 2 / 14n + 2, and caps that add vertices but no pairs.
  • Jemmis: SEP = m + n + o + p for condensed polyhedra and metallocenes; Wade's rule is the case m = 1, o = 0.
Wade (1971); Williams (1971); Mingos (1972, 2021); Jemmis et al. (2001).

1. Predict the shape of B6H10.
12 + 4 = 16 e, 8 pairs = n + 2: nido from a 7-vertex PBP, i.e. a pentagonal pyramid.
2. Why is neutral B6H6 unknown?
It would have 6 pairs for 7 bonding skeletal MOs; one bonding MO would be empty.
