Tricoordinate boron

Two ways to fill an empty orbital

Intermolecular: Al2Cl6

A Cl lone pair of one AlCl3 fills the empty orbital of the other. Each Al becomes tetrahedral and both obtain an octet.

Intramolecular: BF3

Filled F 2pz orbitals have the same symmetry as the empty B 2pz. Overlap (π-donation) reduces the positive charge on B, so the monomer survives.

And BH3?

H has no lone pairs and no π-type orbital, so intramolecular "neutralisation" is impossible. BH3 dimerises exothermically to B2H6, and does so through B–H bonds rather than lone pairs.

E. L. Muetterties, in The Chemistry of Boron and its Compounds, Wiley, 1967, ch. 1, §1.3, pp. 4–5; Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3.
This is Muetterties's argument. Boron is small, so 2p–2p overlap with F is efficient; Al is larger, and its 3p overlap with Cl 3p is poorer, so dimerisation wins. The BH3 case leads straight into the rest of the deck: dimerisation must use the electrons of B–H bonds.
Tricoordinate boron

The π system of BF3

  • σ framework: three B(sp2)–F bonds in the molecular plane.
  • π set: four pz orbitals, one empty on B and three filled on F, perpendicular to the plane.
  • They combine to give one bonding a2″ MO delocalised over all four atoms, two non-bonding e″ MOs on F alone, and one antibonding a2″*.
  • Six π electrons fill a2″ + e″. One π pair is shared over three B–F bonds, so each B–F has a bond order of about 1⅓.

Same pattern elsewhere

BF3 is isoelectronic with CO3²⁻ and NO3⁻, which have the same delocalised four-centre π system.

Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3 (delocalised π orbitals in BX3); Muetterties (1967), ch. 1, p. 4; Mingos (2021), p. 4, Fig. 1.
Build the diagram in steps: σ framework, then the p orbitals, then the bonding π combination. Emphasise that the π electrons come from fluorine, so this is donation into the empty boron orbital. Bond order 1⅓ explains the 22 pm shortening.

Tricoordinate boron

The Lewis acidity paradox: BF3 < BCl3 < BBr3 < BI3

  • Naive expectation: F is the most electronegative and smallest halogen, so BF3 should be the strongest acid.
  • Observed: the order is reversed. BF3 is the weakest of the four.
  • Reorganisation: to accept a base, BX3 must pyramidalise (120° → ≈ 109.5°). That breaks the π overlap, which costs most for BF3.
  • π-donation weakens down the group as 2p–np overlap worsens (F > Cl > Br > I), so the penalty for adduct formation falls and acidity rises.
Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3 ("the reverse of that predicted when electronegativity and size are considered"); Muetterties (1967), ch. 1, p. 5.
Run the animation: the planar molecule bends to tetrahedral as the base approaches, and the π lobes lose alignment. The energy cost of this change is the reorganisation energy. It is largest when π-bonding is strongest, in BF3.
Tricoordinate boron · aside

Is π-donation the whole story?

Computational analyses

  • The energy to pyramidalise BX3 is larger for BF3, but this alone does not fully reproduce the trend.
  • The B charge and LUMO energy also matter: the BCl3 LUMO is lower and more accessible to a donor.
  • With weak bases the order can change. Acidity depends on the partner.

Teaching position: use π-donation as the qualitative explanation, but present it as one of several factors, not the only cause.

This mirrors Muetterties' 1967 caution: the data fit π-bonding, but they may also reflect "some other effect or effects".

T. Brinck, J. S. Murray, P. Politzer, Inorg. Chem. 1993, 32, 2622; F. Bessac, G. Frenking, Inorg. Chem. 2003, 42, 7990; Muetterties (1967), ch. 1, p. 5.
Optional for M.Sc. Mention that the BF3/BCl3 acidity question has a modern computational literature. The point for students is scientific humility: a neat qualitative model is useful, but should not be stated as the whole truth.
Tricoordinate boron

BH3 never survives alone

2 BH3 ⇌ B2H6

ΔH(dissociation) ≈ 150 kJ mol⁻¹

  • Free BH3 has no intramolecular way to fill its pz orbital.
  • It is trapped by any Lewis base: H3B·THF, H3B·SMe2 and H3B·NMe3 are the everyday "BH3" reagents.
  • Without a base it dimerises. The donor is a B–H bond pair, the only electrons BH3 has.

Walsh's idea (1947) is the conceptual bridge to the next part. Each BH3 uses one of its B–H bonds as a donor toward the empty orbital of the other, giving two B–H→B interactions, the two bridges. This is a dative picture of a three-centre bond.
Did you know?

BH3 binds carbon monoxide, like a metal

  • In 1937 A. B. Burg and H. I. Schlesinger made H3B·CO by heating B2H6 with CO under pressure.
  • It was one of the first carbonyls of a non-metal. CO donates its carbon lone pair into the empty p orbital of BH3.
  • B2H6 + 2 CO ⇌ 2 H3B·CO. The equilibrium lies on the left at ordinary pressures, showing that the B–H–B bridges are strong.

Part B

Three centres, two electrons

n atomic orbitals give n molecular orbitals. With three centres, only one may be bonding.

B
Part B develops the general three-centre MO picture using H3+ as the model, before applying it to diborane.
Three-centre bonding

Recap: the two-centre, two-electron bond

  • Two 1s orbitals combine to give two MOs: in-phase σg (bonding) and out-of-phase σu* (antibonding).
  • Two electrons fill σg: bond order = ½(2 − 0) = 1.
  • Rule: n AOs → n MOs. Only the bonding MOs should be filled for maximum stability.

Question

What happens with three AOs but only two electrons?

Standard MO theory; e.g. Wade (1971), p. 18 ("a set of n atomic orbitals … gives rise to a set of n molecular orbitals").
Quick revision. The question at the end sets up H3+.
Three-centre bonding

Three orbitals, two arrangements: open and closed

Open (linear, βAC = 0): energies α + √2β, α, α − √2β. One bonding, one non-bonding, one antibonding MO.

Closed (triangular, all β equal): α + 2β, then a degenerate pair at α − β. One strongly bonding MO and two antibonding.

Hückel treatment as in W. H. Eberhardt, B. Crawford, W. N. Lipscomb, J. Chem. Phys. 1954, 22, 989; Muetterties (1967), ch. 1, pp. 7–8; α = Coulomb integral, β (< 0) = resonance integral.
Build both diagrams. In both cases exactly one MO is bonding, so two electrons is the natural count for a three-centre bond. The closed case binds more strongly (2β versus √2β), which is why B–B–B units in the higher boranes prefer closed, triangular arrangements.

Did you know?

The universe's most important 3c–2e molecule

  • 1989: infrared emission from H3⁺ was detected in the auroras of Jupiter.
  • 1996: H3⁺ was found in interstellar clouds. It drives much interstellar chemistry by protonating other molecules (H3⁺ + X → HX⁺ + H2).
  • Its line intensities act as a thermometer for planetary upper atmospheres, and its abundance helps date star-forming clouds.
P. Drossart et al., Nature 1989, 340, 539 (Jupiter); T. R. Geballe, T. Oka, Nature 1996, 384, 334 (interstellar); T. Oka, PNAS 2006, 103, 12235.
H3+ is the protonated hydrogen molecule. It is the most abundant molecular ion in many astrophysical environments, and a 3c–2e species.

Bonding in B2H6

What any bonding model must explain

  • B–Ht 119 pm, normal, but B–Hb 133 pm, longer.
  • ∠HtBHt 122°, wider than tetrahedral.
  • ∠B–Hb–B only 83°, acute.
  • Diamagnetic: all 12 electrons paired.
  • Stable to 2 BH3 by ≈ 150 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Bridging H are hydridic, not acidic.
Wade (1971), §2-3, pp. 14–15 and Fig. 2-6; hydridic character: Longuet-Higgins, Q. Rev. 1957, 11, 124.
This is the checklist. Return to it at the end of Part C and tick each item against the 3c–2e model.
Bonding in B2H6

Three descriptions that fell short

Four one-electron links

Core (1927). Why would one-electron bonds be so rare elsewhere, always come in pairs here, and have paired spins (diamagnetism)?

Electrostatic BH3···BH3

Two polar Bδ+–Hδ− bonds attracting each other. But B and H differ in electronegativity by only 0.1 (Pauling scale), so the bonds are barely polar.

Resonance hybrids

Covalent and ionic canonical forms can reproduce the count, but it is hard to weigh them, the geometries are wrong, and they "obscure the precise form of the bridge bonding".

The way forward

Pitzer's protonated double bond (1945) treated the bridging electrons as shared by both borons and both bridging H. Longuet-Higgins turned this into partial molecular orbitals.

Wade (1971), pp. 15–17 (one-electron links; electrostatic model; resonance; Pitzer); Longuet-Higgins, Q. Rev. 1957, 11, 123–124.
Wade discusses all three and explains why each fails. Linnett later noted that the one-electron picture does give a good image of where the bonding density is; the problem is spin pairing, which needs electron-pair orbitals.
Bonding in B2H6 · localised view

Localised view, step 1: four ordinary B–Ht bonds

  • Treat each B as sp3-hybridised: four hybrids per B.
  • Two hybrids per B form normal 2c–2e B–Ht bonds: 4 bonds × 2 e = 8 e.
  • Left over: 4 boron hybrids pointing into the bridge, 2 H 1s orbitals and 4 electrons.
  • Split the bridge into two halves: each B–Hb–B unit has 3 orbitals and 2 electrons.
Wade (1971), p. 17; Muetterties (1967), ch. 1, p. 7.
This partitioning is an approximation, but a useful one: it isolates the three-centre problem we have just solved for H3+, now in its open form.
Bonding in B2H6 · localised view

Step 2: the three MOs of one B–H–B unit

ψ1 = ½φB1 + ½φB2 + (1/√2)φH  bonding
ψ2 = (1/√2)(φB1 − φB2)  non-bonding
ψ3 = ½φB1 + ½φB2 − (1/√2)φH  antibonding

  • The H 1s cannot mix into ψ2: it is symmetric about the mirror plane through H, but ψ2 is antisymmetric.
  • Only ψ1 is bonding. Two electrons fill it: one 3c–2e bond.
  • More electrons would enter ψ2, which is B···B antibonding, and weaken the bridge.
Wade (1971), pp. 17–18, Figs 2-8 and 2-9 (redrawn); coefficients: Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.4.1, Fig. 13.4.3; Muetterties (1967), ch. 1, Fig. 1.1.
Build the correlation diagram: first the weak B–B interaction gives σ(BB) and σ*(BB); only σ(BB) has the symmetry to mix with H 1s; that mixing gives ψ1 and ψ3, while σ*(BB) stays behind as ψ2. This is the open three-centre case from Part B.
Bonding in B2H6 · localised view

Two "banana" bonds hold the bridge

  • Each bridge pair occupies a banana-shaped orbital curving from B through Hb to B.
  • Electron count: 4 × B–Ht (8 e) + 2 × B–Hb–B (4 e) = 12 e, with all six bonding orbitals full.
  • An average of one electron per B–Hb link matches the longer B–Hb (133 pm), which is effectively a half bond.
  • Only two orbitals hold the four bridge electrons, so their spins are paired, as diamagnetism requires.

Analogy

This is like describing ethylene's C=C as two bent ("banana") bonds from sp3 carbons, instead of σ + π.

Wade (1971), pp. 18–19, Fig. 2-10 (redrawn); H. C. Longuet-Higgins, J. Chim. Phys. 1949, 46, 268; Longuet-Higgins, Q. Rev. 1957, 11, 125.
Connect back to Core (Deck 1): his four one-electron links describe the same electron density, but placing two electrons in each of two orbitals pairs the spins. This is why the three-centre description succeeded where the one-electron picture failed.
Bonding in B2H6 · delocalised view

Pitzer's picture as MOs: two four-centre bridge orbitals

  • Take B as sp2, which also fits ∠HtBHt = 122°. The bridge then has a σ(B–B) combination of sp2 hybrids and a π(B–B) combination of p orbitals.
  • The two bridge H give s′ + s″ (same symmetry as σ) and s′ − s″ (same symmetry as π).
  • Matching symmetries combine into two bonding MOs, a = σ + (s′ + s″) and b = π + (s′ − s″), each holding 2 e.
  • Both are B···B bonding. Only a is H···H bonding, which explains the acute B–H–B angle.
Longuet-Higgins, Q. Rev. 1957, 11, 124–125, Fig. 1; Wade (1971), pp. 19–20, Figs 2-11 to 2-13 (redrawn); K. S. Pitzer, J. Am. Chem. Soc. 1945, 67, 1126.
This is Pitzer's protonated double bond with the hydrogen 1s orbitals included. MO a is the σ part of the "double bond" with both protons in phase; MO b is the π part with the protons out of phase. Note that b is the bridge-only b3u orbital we will meet in the full MO scheme.
Bonding in B2H6 · localised = delocalised

Two descriptions, one electron density

  • Two doubly occupied MOs a and b can be replaced by their combinations (a ± b)/√2 without changing the total wavefunction or energy.
  • (a + b)/√2 concentrates on the upper bridge, and (a − b)/√2 on the lower.
  • These are exactly the two 3c–2e banana bonds.

Take-home

"Two 3c–2e bonds" and "two four-centre MOs" are equivalent. Choose whichever helps: localised for counting, delocalised for spectroscopy.

Longuet-Higgins, Q. Rev. 1957, 11, 125 (equivalence of localised and delocalised orbitals, after Lennard-Jones 1949); Wade (1971), p. 21.
Run the animation: a + b reinforces at the top hydrogen and cancels at the bottom; a − b does the reverse. This orbital-transformation principle is general and worth stating explicitly for M.Sc. students.
Bonding in B2H6 · full MO treatment

The full treatment: 14 valence AOs in D2h

  • AOs: 2 × (B 2s, 2px, 2py, 2pz) + 6 × H 1s = 14, giving 14 MOs.
  • Group them into symmetry orbitals (axes: z along B···B, bridge H in the xz plane, terminal H in the yz plane).
  • Only orbitals of the same symmetry mix. This gives 6 bonding MOs for 12 electrons.

Which bonds does each bonding MO make?

ag (two): terminal and bridge · b1u, b2u, b3g: terminal only · b3u: bridge only, Pitzer's protonated π bond.

Bonding in B2H6 · experiment

Testing the MOs: photoelectron spectra of B2H6 and C2H4

  • PES: He I photons (21.2 eV) eject valence electrons, and I = hν − Ekinetic gives each MO's ionisation energy.
  • B2H6 and C2H4 are isoelectronic. Their MOs correlate one-to-one, which supports Pitzer's analogy.
  • In C2H4 the π orbital (1b3u) is the HOMO, at ≈ 10.5 eV. In B2H6 the embedded protons stabilise it to ≈ 14.7 eV.
  • The HOMO of B2H6 is 1b3g, a terminal B–H orbital. Ionisation therefore weakens the terminal bonds.
Correlation after Wade (1971), Fig. 2-17, from C. R. Brundle, M. B. Robin, H. Basch, M. Pinsky, A. Bond, J. Am. Chem. Soc. 1970, 92, 3863. B2H6 energies read from Wade's figure (± 0.3 eV); C2H4: standard PES values. Wade's text quotes the He II line (40.8 eV).
This is the experimental test of the MO picture. The striking feature is the large stabilisation of the b3u (π-type) orbital: in ethylene it is the highest occupied orbital, in diborane it lies below three σ orbitals. The bridging protons sit inside this orbital's density, exactly as Pitzer proposed.

Verdict

Pauling's objection was a sound observation. The 3c–2e bond contains the B–B bonding he detected, through the boron end of each banana bond.

Students often think the bridge has no B–B component. The data say otherwise. This also explains why the B–H–B angle is so acute.

Beyond diborane

Electron-poor 3c–2e versus electron-rich 3c–4e

3c–2e (B–H–B, Al–C–Al): only the bonding MO is filled. Each link has bond order ½. The bridge atom brings one orbital and one electron.

3c–4e (XeF2, I3⁻, HF2⁻; halide bridges in Al2Cl6): the non-bonding MO is filled too. Each link still has bond order ½, but the system is electron-rich.

3c–4e model: G. C. Pimentel, J. Chem. Phys. 1951, 19, 446; R. E. Rundle (1940s–60s); contrast drawn in Wade (1971), ch. 1.
Same three MOs, different filling. In 3c–2e the non-bonding MO is empty; in 3c–4e it is full, with its density on the outer atoms (which is why XeF2 needs electronegative ligands). A useful exam comparison.

Summary

Key points

  • BX3: planar; X→B π-donation (a2″) shortens B–X and makes BF3 the weakest acid. It is a useful model with known limits.
  • BH3 cannot π-donate, so it dimerises using its B–H bonds as donors (Walsh).
  • Three AOs give one bonding MO: closed (α + 2β) or open (α + √2β). Two electrons is the natural count.
  • B2H6: 4 × 2c–2e (B–Ht) + 2 × 3c–2e (B–H–B) = 12 e. This is equivalent to two four-centre MOs (a, b).
  • Full D2h MOs: 6 bonding (2 ag, b1u, b2u, b3g, b3u). PES confirms the correlation with C2H4 and the stabilised b3u.
  • 3c–2e (bonding MO only) versus 3c–4e (bonding + non-bonding filled).
Wade (1971), §2-3; Longuet-Higgins (1957); Muetterties (1967), ch. 1; Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §§13.3–13.4.
Ask students to draw the B–H–B MO diagram from memory and state which orbital is filled.
Check your understanding

Questions (press → to reveal each answer)

1. Why is BCl3 a stronger Lewis acid than BF3?
B–F π-donation is strongest. Forming an adduct pyramidalises boron and destroys that π bonding, which costs most for BF3.
2. Why can H 1s not mix into ψ2 of the B–H–B unit?
ψ2 is antisymmetric to the mirror plane through H, while H 1s is symmetric, so their overlap is zero.
3. Why is H3⁺ triangular rather than linear?
The closed arrangement has a lower bonding MO (α + 2β against α + √2β) for the same two electrons.
4. What does the PES of B2H6 show about the b3u orbital?
It is strongly stabilised (≈ 14.7 eV, against ≈ 10.5 eV in C2H4) by the bridging protons embedded in it, as Pitzer's model predicts.
Based on Wade (1971), §2-3, and this deck.
