Part A

Why a counting scheme?

By the mid-1950s the structures were known. What was missing was a way to write valence structures for them.

A
Part A sets the historical problem and the building blocks.
Why a counting scheme? · history

W. N. Lipscomb and the borane problem

  • From 1946 his group grew single crystals of volatile boranes at low temperature and solved them by X-ray: B5H9, B4H10, B5H11, B6H10, and the new B9H15.
  • The structures formed "a bewildering group". Most looked like fragments of an icosahedron, but no rule linked formula to bonding.
  • 1954, with Eberhardt and Crawford: treat boranes with localised 2c–2e and 3c–2e bonds. 1957, with Dickerson: the "semi-topological" equations of balance.
  • Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 1976, "for his studies on the structure of boranes illuminating problems of chemical bonding".
Mingos (2021), §4, pp. 11–17; W. H. Eberhardt, B. Crawford, W. N. Lipscomb, J. Chem. Phys. 1954, 22, 989; R. E. Dickerson, W. N. Lipscomb, J. Chem. Phys. 1957, 27, 212; Nobel citation: nobelprize.org. Portrait: Wikimedia Commons.
Lipscomb combined difficult low-temperature crystallography on air-sensitive liquids with bonding theory. His own aim, as Mingos quotes, was the "intuitive feeling for the chemical bonding" that comes from localising electron pairs.
Why a counting scheme? · the idea

Balance atoms, orbitals and electrons

Langmuir (1919): octet molecules

  • Each of n atoms needs an octet: 8n electrons.
  • Each shared pair counts twice, so e = 8n − 2p, where e is the number of valence electrons and p the number of shared pairs.
  • The unknowns are 2c–2e bonds and lone pairs.

Lipscomb (1954–57): boranes

  • Each B still uses all four valence orbitals: the octet survives.
  • There are no lone pairs. Instead there are two kinds of 3c–2e bond as well as 2c–2e bonds.
  • The unknowns become the numbers s, t, y, x, fixed by balancing H atoms, orbitals and electron pairs.

Same logic, new bond types

Lipscomb's equations "resembled those which Langmuir had successfully developed earlier". The novelty lies only in admitting three-centre bonds into the count.

Mingos (2021), p. 15; I. Langmuir, J. Am. Chem. Soc. 1919, 41, 868; Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.4.2 (bond valence b = ½(8n − g)).
This connects back to Deck 1, where Lewis and Langmuir could not write an octet structure for B2H6. Lipscomb keeps the octet idea and enlarges the set of allowed bonds.
Why a counting scheme? · building blocks

Five bonding components, four letters

Mingos (2021), Fig. 8, p. 13; Das, Fig. 10.12.5.2; Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.4.1. Open and closed B–B–B bonds were merged into a single t by I. R. Epstein, W. N. Lipscomb, Inorg. Chem. 1971, 10, 1921.
Every terminal B–H is a normal 2c–2e bond; x counts only the extra terminal B–H bonds beyond one per boron (so x is the number of BH2 groups). The open B–B–B bond uses a p orbital on the central boron; the closed bond uses one hybrid from each boron directed to the triangle centre. Later SCF work showed the distinction is not essential, so both are counted in t.
Why a counting scheme? · assumptions

The ground rules

What is assumed

  • Every boron carries at least one terminal H, so the formula can be written BpHp+q = (BH)pHq.
  • Every boron uses four valence orbitals in exactly four bonds (2c or 3c).
  • Every pair of neighbouring B atoms is joined by a B–B, B–H–B or B–B–B bond.

Where this leads

Each BH unit then has 3 orbitals and 2 electrons left for the framework. Each extra H has 1 orbital and 1 electron. This is exactly the bookkeeping that, after 1971, became Wade's skeletal-electron count (Deck 6).

Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.4.1, rules (a)–(c); Wade (1971), §3-3, p. 41.
Stress the first assumption: it fails for B10H16 or B20H16, where some boron atoms carry no terminal H. We correct for this later with an extra parameter r.
Part B

The equations of balance

Three counts, four unknowns, and a range of allowed solutions.

B
Part B: deriving and solving the equations.
The equations of balance

Three balances for (BH)pHq

1 · Hydrogen balance

The q extra H atoms are either bridges or extra terminal H:

s + x = q

2 · Orbital balance

Each B brings 4 orbitals but only 3 electrons: one "spare" orbital per B, absorbed by one 3c bond each:

s + t = p

3 · Electron-pair balance

Framework pairs available, p + q/2, fill the s, t, y, x bonds:

s + t + y + x = p + q/2

Rearranged (Wade's form)

x = q − s
t = p − s
y = ½(s − x)

  • Four unknowns, three equations: choose s, and t, y, x follow.
  • Only solutions with t, y, x ≥ 0 (and integer y) are physical.
Wade (1971), §3-3, eqns (3-1)–(3-3), pp. 41–42; Das, §10.12.5; Mingos (2021), p. 15.
Derivation of the electron count: each BH unit supplies 2 electrons (one pair) to the framework after its terminal B–H bond is formed; each extra H supplies one electron, i.e. half a pair. So the framework has p + q/2 pairs, and each of the s, t, y, x bonds uses one pair. Subtracting equation 2 from equation 3 gives y + x = q/2 ... combined with equation 1 gives y = (s − x)/2.
The equations of balance

How many bridges?  q/2 ≤ s ≤ q

  • Upper limit. x = q − s ≥ 0, so s ≤ q. There cannot be more bridges than extra H atoms.
  • Lower limit. y = ½(s − x) ≥ 0 means s ≥ x, i.e. s ≥ q/2.
  • Third limit. t = p − s ≥ 0, so s ≤ p.
  • So only a handful of values need checking, even for large boranes.

Das writes the condition as q/2 ≤ s ≤ q. With the extra condition s ≤ p, diborane has only one candidate. Note that s − x = 2s − q, which is always even when q is even, so y is always an integer for neutral boranes.

The equations of balance · selection

Choosing among solutions: Lipscomb's rules

  1. Symmetry. Every known borane has at least a two-fold element (mirror plane, C2 axis or centre). Low symmetry marks a reactive site.
  2. No two adjacent B may be joined by both a closed and an open three-centre bond (their orbitals would be 90° apart).
  3. A B bound to five other B carries one terminal H and no bridge or BH2. Bridges and BH2 groups therefore lie on the edges of the framework.
  4. A B bound to four other B probably uses no more than one B–H–B bridge.
  5. A B bound to only two other B is involved in at least one B–H–B bridge.

Plus one from Li, Zhou and Mak

No two B may be joined by both a 2c B–B bond and a 3c B–B–B or B–H–B bond.

Wade (1971), §3-3, p. 44, rules (1)–(5) after Lipscomb, Boron Hydrides (1963); Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.4.1, rule (d).
These are empirical. They were derived from the known structures and then used to judge hypothetical ones. Rule 3 explains why bridges sit around the open face (Deck 3).
The equations of balance · worked example

Tetraborane(10): 4012, not 3103

4012  ✓ observed

4 bridges, 1 B–B bond, 2 BH2. C2v: the butterfly of Deck 3.

3103  ✗ rejected

3 bridges, 1 B–B–B, 3 BH2. Fits the equations but has no symmetry element (rule 1).

B4H10 = (BH)4H6: p = 4, q = 6. Of the seven trial values s = 0–6, only s = 3 and 4 give non-negative t, y, x.

Wade (1971), pp. 43–44 and Fig. 3-3; Das, §10.12.6, Fig. 10.12.6.1b; structure: Nordman & Lipscomb, J. Chem. Phys. 1953, 21, 1856.
Check the 3103 drawing: each boron still uses four orbitals, so it is a valid valence structure, but it is unsymmetrical. Lipscomb's symmetry rule removes it.

The equations of balance · a limitation

B5H9: one 4120 drawing is not enough

The four equivalent 4120 canonical forms (apex at centre, basal square around it).

  • Any single 4120 form makes the apex–base links unequal: two 2c B–B bonds and one B–B–B bond.
  • Experiment (microwave, X-ray): all four apex–base distances are equal, 1.69 Å; basal edges 1.80 Å. C4v.
  • Fix 1: resonance among four canonical forms.
  • Fix 2: a partial MO treatment. Four basal sp3 hybrids and three apical orbitals (sp + 2 p) give 7 framework MOs, of which 3 are bonding and filled.

Scaling up

B10H14 needs 24 canonical forms to reproduce its C2v symmetry.

Wade (1971), §3-4, Figs. 3-14 and 3-15, pp. 47–49; H. J. Hrostowski, R. J. Myers, J. Chem. Phys. 1954, 22, 262; Das, §10.12.6 (resonance); Mingos (2021), p. 17.
This is the first sign of the method's main limitation: for symmetrical, compact cages the localised description needs many resonance forms. The MO description, with 3 bonding framework orbitals, is simpler and leads towards Wade's rules.

Decaborane(14): 4620

  • (BH)10H4: p = 10, q = 4. With s = 4: t = 6, y = 2, x = 0.
  • Four bridges on the open face; six three-centre B–B–B bonds and two B–B bonds hold the basket together.

Did you know?

Boranes trained two more Nobel laureates

  • Roald Hoffmann did his Ph.D. in Lipscomb's group. The extended Hückel method, developed there by Lohr and Hoffmann to treat boranes, later carried Hoffmann into organic and organometallic chemistry (Nobel Prize 1981).
  • Thomas Steitz, another Lipscomb Ph.D., used X-ray crystallography to solve the large ribosomal subunit (Nobel Prize 2009).
  • Lipscomb's own group moved on from boranes to the structures of enzymes such as carboxypeptidase A.
Biographical: W. N. Lipscomb, Wikipedia (accessed Oct 2026); Mingos (2021), p. 17; R. Hoffmann, J. Chem. Phys. 1963, 39, 1397. Portraits: Wikimedia Commons.
A good story about how a narrow problem (bonding in boranes) produced general tools: the extended Hückel method was used for decades across chemistry.

Wade (1971), eqns (3-7)–(3-9) and Fig. 3-13, pp. 46–47; Das, §10.12.6 (B2H7⁻, B3H8⁻); X-ray: C. R. Peters, C. E. Nordman, J. Am. Chem. Soc. 1960, 82, 5758.
Note the sign convention: c is the charge, so c = −1 for these anions, and the formula has p + q + c hydrogens. Removing H+ from a neutral borane does not change the framework electron count, which is why B3H8− behaves like a B3H9 arachno species.
Extending the scheme

Predicting unknown boranes: B3H9 and B3H7

B3H9: only 3003

Three BH2 groups, a bridge on every edge. A likely first intermediate in diborane pyrolysis (Deck 3).

B3H7: 2102 or 3011

2102 drawn. Both forms need strained bond angles at boron, consistent with B3H7 being a short-lived intermediate.

Procedure: compute all non-negative styx sets, draw each as a network that obeys the rules, and judge the survivors by symmetry and bond angles.

Wade (1971), §3-3, p. 44, Fig. 3-12; Lipscomb, Boron Hydrides (1963), ch. 2.
This is how Lipscomb used the scheme as a predictive tool. Ask students to verify that 3003 is the only solution for B3H9: p = 3, q = 6, so s = 3 is forced by s ≥ q/2 = 3 and s ≤ p = 3.
Extending the scheme

Borons without terminal hydrogen: the parameter r

  • In B10H16, B18H22 and B20H16 some borons carry no terminal H: write Bp+rHp+q.
  • Modified equations:
    x = q − s;  t = p + r − s;  y = ½(s + r − x)

Example: B10H16 = two B5H8 units joined apex to apex

p = 8, r = 2, q = 8 → s = 8, t = 2, y = 5, x = 0: (8250).

Check: twice B5H9 (4120) gives 8240, plus one extra 2c B–B bond for the link: 8250 ✓

Wade (1971), eqns (3-4)–(3-6) and Table 3-2, pp. 45–46.
Fully general form, for charge c as well: x = q + c − s; t = p + r + c − s; y = ½(s + r − 3c − x). Everything earlier is the special case r = c = 0.
Extending the scheme

Closo cages BnHn²⁻: always y = 3

  • No bridges and no BH2: s = x = 0.
  • Framework pairs: t + y = n + 1. Bond valence: 2t + y = 2n − 1.
  • Hence t = n − 2 and y = 3: e.g. B6H6²⁻ is 0430, B12H12²⁻ is 0,10,3,0.
  • B4H4²⁻ (0230) cannot be drawn on a tetrahedron without breaking rule (d), and the series starts at n = 5.

Note

Again many canonical forms are needed; in closo cages the localised picture is least natural.

Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.4.4, eqns (13.4.4)–(13.4.9), Table 13.4.2, Fig. 13.4.7.
The bond valence b = ½(8n − g): for BnHn2−, g = 4n + 2 (three from each B, one from each H, plus 2 for the charge... framework only), so b = 2n − 1. Each 3c bond has bond valence 2, each 2c bond 1.

The bridge to Wade's rules

Equation 3 already counts the skeletal electron pairs:

S = s + t + y + x = p + q/2

classqskeletal pairs Sexample
closo2p + 1B6H6²⁻ (as B6H8): 7
nido4p + 2B5H9: 7
arachno6p + 3B4H10: 7
hypho8p + 4B3H11 (hypothetical): 7
  • B6H6²⁻, B5H9 and B4H10 all have 7 pairs: the same octahedral parent (Deck 3).
  • Lipscomb showed in 1979 that the styx equations and Wade's rules are equivalent counts.
  • What Wade added was the link to shape: n + 1 pairs → closo deltahedron of n vertices (Deck 6).
W. N. Lipscomb, Inorg. Chem. 1979, 18, 2328; Mingos (2021), p. 17; Das, §10.12.6 (S = p + q/2).
This is the key conceptual bridge to Deck 6. The same arithmetic that fixes styx also gives the number of framework electron pairs.
Assessment

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

  • Keeps the familiar language of localised bonds and the octet.
  • An exact audit of atoms, orbitals and electron pairs.
  • Sharply limits the candidate structures, and was used to judge hypothetical boranes.
  • Lipscomb later recast accurate SCF wavefunctions into these same localised components (Boys localisation).

Limitations

  • Solutions are often not unique; chemistry must choose.
  • The same code can be drawn in several ways (open or closed B–B–B).
  • Symmetrical cages need many resonance forms (4 for B5H9, 24 for B10H14).
  • It says nothing about the three-dimensional shape.
Das, §10.12.6, "Limitations of styx number method"; Mingos (2021), pp. 16–17.
Mingos summarises: an accurate audit of the electron pairs, but not a full three-dimensional description. That gap is what Williams and Wade filled in 1971.
Summary

Key points

  • s B–H–B bridges, t three-centre B–B–B bonds, y B–B bonds, x extra B–H (BH2 groups).
  • For (BH)pHq: s + x = q;  s + t = p;  s + t + y + x = p + q/2.
  • Allowed codes need non-negative t, y, x. Symmetry, NMR and Lipscomb's rules choose among them.
  • Key codes: B2H6 2002, B4H10 4012, B5H9 4120, B5H11 3203, B6H10 4220, B10H14 4620; closo BnHn²⁻ = 0,(n−2),3,0.
  • s + t + y + x is the skeletal electron-pair count used by Wade.
Wade (1971), §3-3; Das, §10.12.5–6; Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.4; Mingos (2021), §4.
Ask students to write the styx code for B6H10 from memory and check it with the equations.
