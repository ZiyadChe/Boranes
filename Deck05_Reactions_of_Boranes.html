Part A

Oxidation and solvolysis

Terminal B–H hydrogen is hydridic: oxygen, water and alcohols remove it.

A
Part A: the reactions that make boranes hazardous.
Oxidation and solvolysis

Burning and hydrolysis

With oxygen

B2H6 + 3 O2 → B2O3 + 3 H2O

  • Heats of combustion are about 1.5 times those of comparable hydrocarbons (Deck 3, zip fuels).
  • High concentrations of B2H6 in air oxidise explosively; B5H9 ignites spontaneously.

With water and alcohols

B2H6 + 6 H2O → 2 B(OH)3 + 6 H2
B4H10 + 12 H2O → 4 B(OH)3 + 11 H2

  • Each B–H gives one H2: Hδ− on boron meets Hδ+ of water.
  • Methanol does the same, giving B(OMe)3.
  • Rates vary greatly: B10H14 is attacked only slowly, and closo B10H10²⁻ and B12H12²⁻ are kinetically stable to hydrolysis.
Das, §10.13.2(a)–(c); Hawthorne (1967), §5.5C, F; Stone (1955).
Balance check for B4H10: 10 H on boron plus 24 H in water = 34 H; 12 in boric acid (4 × 3 OH), leaving 22 H = 11 H2. The kinetic stability of the closo anions is a recurring theme (Deck 7).

  • B2H5I couples like an alkyl halide in a Wurtz reaction:

The Wurtz-type coupling shows that B2H5 behaves like an alkyl group. It is also a way of making a B–B bond.

  • Each B keeps three H; the base fills the vacancy left by the broken bridge.
  • Ligand displacement follows base strength, e.g. R3P + Me3N·BH3 → R3P·BH3 + Me3N, by SN2 or SN1 paths depending on steric crowding.

Borane carbonyl

H3B·CO has a linear B–C–O unit and a tetrahedral boron. It is isoelectronic with CH3CO⁺. No CO adduct of a trigonal BX3 is known; hyperconjugative B–H → CO π* donation is thought to stabilise it.

Hawthorne (1967), §5.5A, pp. 244–245, and §5.5C, p. 251; Das, §10.13.2(d)(i).
Most common outcome for soft or bulky bases. The adducts THF·BH3 and Me2S·BH3 are how chemists actually handle "BH3" (Deck 2).
Lewis bases

Where does the second base go?

Small, hard bases (NH3, OH⁻, MeNH2, Me2SO) attack the same boron again: [L2BH2]⁺[BH4]⁻.

Bulky or soft bases (Me3N, PR3, ethers) are blocked at that boron and attack the other: 2 L·BH3.

Mechanism via a singly bridged intermediate: Parry and Shore, as discussed in Hawthorne (1967), §5.5C, pp. 251–252; steric and "symbiotic" rationale: Das, §10.13.2(d)(i).
Step 1 is common to both: one base opens one bridge, giving L–BH2–H–BH3. Step 2 decides. Das's term "symbiosis" refers to the hard–hard preference: a boron already carrying a hard N donor accepts a second hard donor.
Lewis bases · history

A long puzzle: the "diammoniate of diborane"

  • Stock found that B2H6 and NH3 combine at low temperature to give a salt-like solid, B2H6·2NH3.
  • Its formulation was argued over for decades: [NH4]2[B2H4]? NH4[H3B–NH2–BH3]?
  • Parry and co-workers (1958) settled it as the first unsymmetric cleavage product: [H2B(NH3)2]⁺[BH4]⁻.
  • Other examples followed: Me2SO, Me2NH and MeNH2 give [BH2L2][BH4].

Aside: ammonia borane

The symmetric-cleavage product of the same pair, H3N·BH3, is isoelectronic with ethane but a solid. It holds 19.6 wt % hydrogen and has been studied extensively as a chemical hydrogen-storage material.

Hawthorne (1967), §5.5C, p. 251 (Stock; Parry, ref. 101; Shore, refs. 103–104); S. G. Shore, R. W. Parry, J. Am. Chem. Soc. 1958, 80, 8. H content of NH3BH3: 6 × 1.008 / 30.87.
This is a good example of how hard it was to characterise these compounds before NMR. The 19.6 percent value is simple arithmetic: six H atoms in a formula mass of 30.87.
Lewis bases

Cleaving B4H10: the same choice, bigger fragments

Symmetric (L = Me3N, R2O, Me3P):
B4H10 + 2 L → L·B3H7 + L·BH3

Unsymmetric (L = NH3, OH⁻):
B4H10 + 2 L → [H2BL2]⁺[B3H8]⁻

NMR at −53 °C in THF shows B3H8⁻ first, even though B3H7·THF is isolated. Unsymmetric cleavage may always occur first; with weak ligands, B3H8⁻ then transfers H⁻ to [H2BL2]⁺.

Hawthorne (1967), §5.5D and §5.5F, pp. 254–258, Fig. 5.13 (Schaeffer, Tebbe and Phillips); Das, §10.13.2(d)(i).
The B3H8− ion (styx 2013, Deck 4) is fluxional: its 11B NMR shows all eight H equivalent on the NMR time scale.

The rule in one line

Arachno boranes contain BH2 groups joined by bridges. Bases remove one of them as BH3 (symmetric) or BH2⁺ (unsymmetric), leaving a smaller borane or borane anion. Compare the (BH)n(BH3)x picture of Deck 3.

Das, §10.13.2(d)(i).
Students can balance each equation by counting B and H; the charges balance between the cation and the anion.
Lewis bases

Addition without cleavage: B10H12L2

B10H14 + 2 L → B10H12L2 + H2

L = MeCN (Schaeffer, 1957), R2S, R3P, R3N, pyridine …

  • The ligands bind at B6 and B9, on the open face.
  • B10H12L2 is isoelectronic and isostructural with B10H14²⁻: each L replaces an H⁻. Adding two electron pairs opens nido to arachno.
  • Displacement order: R2S = RCN < R2NCN < R2NCOR < R3N < R3P = pyridine.
  • Smaller example: nido-B5H9 + 2 PMe3 → hypho-B5H9(PMe3)2.
Hawthorne (1967), §5.5O.8, pp. 273–276, Fig. 5.21; Das, §10.13.2(d)(iii), Fig. 10.13.2.1.
Use Wade-type counting (Deck 6): B10H14 has 12 skeletal pairs (nido, n + 2); B10H12L2 and B10H14 2− have 13 (arachno, n + 3). The cage opens accordingly.
Lewis bases · cage closure

From B10H12L2 to closo B10H10²⁻ and to carboranes

B10H12L2 + 2 R3N → [R3NH]2[B10H10] + 2 L

With Et3N directly from B10H14: 90–95 % yield.

B10H12(SEt2)2 + HC≡CH → 1,2-C2B10H12 + 2 SEt2 + H2

ortho-carborane (Deck 7).

  • Label tracing: 1,2,3,4-D4-B10H10 puts all D in equatorial positions; 2-Br gives 2-Br-B10H9²⁻. So B6 and B9 swing in to become the two apices.
  • Kinetics: first a ligand dissociates, then the base removes a proton and the cage closes.
  • The arachno cage closes because base removes 2 H⁺ while the ligands leave with their electron pairs.
Hawthorne (1967), §5.6A.2, pp. 283–289, Fig. 5.25; Das, §10.13.2(d)(iii) and Scheme 10.13.2.1.
This sequence links Decks 3, 5 and 7: nido B10H14, arachno B10H12L2, closo B10H10 2− and the icosahedral carborane. Skeletal electron count goes 12 → 13 → 11 pairs; Deck 6 explains why each count gives that shape.

Part C: Brønsted acidity.
Bridging hydrogens as acids

Boranes as Brønsted acids

A 3c–2e B–H–B bond becomes a 2c–2e B–B bond. Electron count and cage are unchanged.

B10H14 + OH⁻ ⇌ B10H13⁻ + H2O  (pKa ≈ 2.7)

  • Titrated as a monoprotic acid; acidifying recovers B10H14. The anion is yellow (λmax 267, 335 nm).
  • D2O/dioxane exchanges only the four bridge H at first: B10H10D4.
  • Stronger bases for weaker acids: B6H10 + KH → K[B6H9] + H2; NaH or Ph3P=CH2 for B10H14.
Das, §10.13.2(d)(ii); Hawthorne (1967), §5.5O.3, pp. 267–268; G. A. Guter, G. W. Schaeffer, J. Am. Chem. Soc. 1956, 78, 3546.
Stock noticed the yellow colour with aqueous hydroxide. The key proof that B10H14 is a Brønsted rather than a Lewis acid came from the titration and the spectrum: the anion is B10H13−, not B10H14OH−.
Bridging hydrogens as acids

Which boranes are the stronger acids?

  • Measured by competition, e.g. B10H14 + B4H9⁻ → B4H10 + B10H13⁻: B10H14 is the stronger acid.
  • Larger boranes are more acidic than smaller ones.
  • Arachno boranes are more acidic than nido boranes of similar size.
  • Reason: a larger cage spreads the negative charge of the conjugate base more widely, as phenoxide is more stable than methoxide.
Das, §10.13.2(d)(ii), acid-strength sequences.
Nido: B5H9 < B6H10 < B10H14 < B16H20. Arachno: B4H10 < B5H11 < B6H12. The diagram shows the two ladders as qualitative orders only; no pKa scale is implied.
Bridging hydrogens as acids

What the conjugate bases are good for

Cage growth

The new B–B bond is electron-rich and inserts BH3:

K[B5H8] + ½ B2H6 → K[B6H11] → (H⁺) B6H10

Deck 3, Part D.

Alkylation

B10H14 + MeMgI → B10H13MgI + CH4, a borane "Grignard reagent". With RX it gives mainly 6-RB10H13.

Metal insertion

The B–B bond can also bind metal fragments in the bridge position, the route to many metallaboranes.

Deck 8.

Base-catalysed rearrangement

Bases convert 1-R-B5H8 into 2-R-B5H8 through the bridge-deprotonated anion; the 2-isomer is the more stable.

Das, §10.12.7(d) and §10.13.2(d)(ii); Hawthorne (1967), §5.5G, p. 261 and §5.5O.4, p. 269.
Deprotonation therefore opens three synthetic doors. The metal insertion route returns in Deck 8.
Part D

Electrophilic and nucleophilic substitution

The charge is not spread evenly over a borane cage, and reagents find the difference.

D
Part D: where on the cage reagents attack.
Substitution

Pentaborane(9): the apex reacts first, the base wins later

  • B5H9 + I2 → 1-IB5H8 + HI; Br2 gives 1-BrB5H8.
  • RCl/AlCl3 (or alkene/AlCl3) gives 1-R; at 200 °C the product is 2-R.
  • DCl/AlCl3 exchanges only the apical H.

Kinetic versus thermodynamic control

The apical boron carries the most negative charge, so electrophiles attack it fastest (1-isomer). The 2-isomer is more stable and forms on heating or with base.

Hawthorne (1967), §5.5G, pp. 260–261; Das, §10.13.2(d), Fig. 10.13.2.2; reactivity of halogens Cl2 > Br2 > I2 (Das).
The analogy with aromatic electrophilic substitution is deliberate: a Friedel–Crafts catalyst, an electron-rich site, and kinetic versus thermodynamic products.

Did you know?

One valence structure predicts the wrong dipole

  • A single localised (styx) structure of B10H14 gives formal charges of −⅓ e at B2 and B4, and a dipole moment of 6.6 D.
  • The measured value is 3.52 D. Lipscomb counted 109 canonical structures for the molecule.
  • A molecular-orbital calculation gave 3.6 D, and the same calculation gave the charges on the previous slide.

Adding electrons and hydride

Reduction and hydride addition

Electrons

B10H14 + 2 Na → Na2[B10H14]
2 Na + 2 B2H6 → NaBH4 + NaB3H8

  • A red intermediate, probably the radical anion B10H14•−, is seen on the way.
  • B10H14²⁻ is arachno: B6 and B9 become BH2 groups, as Lipscomb predicted from its valence structure.

Hydride

2 MH + B2H6 → 2 M[BH4]
NaBH4 + B2H6 → NaB3H8 + H2 (diglyme, 100 °C)
B10H14 + BH4⁻ → B10H15⁻ + ½ B2H6

  • B2H6 + H⁻ also gives the singly bridged B2H7⁻ (Deck 4).
  • NaBH4 in water reduces B10H14 to B10H14²⁻ in high yield.
Hawthorne (1967), §5.5A, §5.5D, §5.5O.7, pp. 244, 252–253, 271–274 (Gaines, Schaeffer and Tebbe for NaB3H8).
Same principle as on the B10H12L2 slide: two extra electrons convert a nido cage to arachno. The NaBH4/B2H6 route is the practical large-scale synthesis of B3H8−.

Hydroboration

The borane reaction that changed organic synthesis.

Part F.
Hydroboration

B–H adds across C=C: syn and anti-Markovnikov

  • BH3 (as THF·BH3) accepts the alkene π electrons; positive charge builds on the more substituted carbon.
  • A hydridic H moves to that carbon through a four-centre transition state, so H and B add to the same face (syn).

"Anti-Markovnikov"?

Das notes that the label is misleading: the negative part of the reagent (H⁻) does go to the more substituted carbon, exactly as Markovnikov's rule requires. Only the atom differs from HX additions.

Das, §10.13.2(e); H. C. Brown, B. C. Subba Rao, J. Am. Chem. Soc. 1956, 78, 5694.
Regiochemistry follows from charge: B (δ+) goes to the less substituted carbon, H (δ−) to the more substituted one. Stereochemistry follows from the four-centre transition state.

Did you know?

Hydroboration began with an odd result

  • At Purdue, B. C. Subba Rao was reducing ethyl oleate with NaBH4/AlCl3 and found that it used more hydride than expected. The C=C bond was reacting too.
  • With H. C. Brown he reported the conversion of alkenes into organoboranes and alcohols in 1956.
  • Brown had begun in inorganic chemistry, working with Schlesinger on diborane and co-discovering NaBH4. He shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
Brown & Subba Rao, J. Am. Chem. Soc. 1956, 78, 5694; Das, §10.13.2(e); ethyl oleate observation: ChemistryViews, profile of H. C. Brown; Subba Rao publication list: Indian Academy of Sciences repository.
An instance of an unexpected stoichiometry leading to a major reaction. B. C. Subba Rao was an Indian chemist and Fellow of the Indian Academy of Sciences.

Summary

Key points

  • Hydridic B–Ht: boranes burn, hydrolyse to B(OH)3 + H2, and are halogenated at terminal positions.
  • Lewis bases cleave B–H–B bridges: symmetric (bulky/soft L → L·BH3) or unsymmetric (small/hard L → [L2BH2]⁺); or they add and open the cage (B10H12L2).
  • Bridging H are protic: B10H14 pKa ≈ 2.7; larger and arachno boranes are stronger acids.
  • Electrophiles attack electron-rich borons (B5H9 apex; B10H14 2,4 > 1,3); nucleophiles attack B6, B9.
  • Adding 2 e⁻ or 2 L turns nido into arachno; base can close B10H14 to B10H10²⁻. Hydroboration adds B–H syn, B to the less substituted carbon.
Das, §10.13.2; Hawthorne (1967), §5.5.
Ask students to classify a new reaction by which of the four structural features it uses.
Check your understanding

Questions (press → to reveal each answer)

1. Predict the products of B2H6 with (a) PMe3, (b) NH3.
(a) 2 Me3P·BH3 (symmetric). (b) [H2B(NH3)2][BH4] (unsymmetric).
2. Why is B10H14 a stronger acid than B4H10?
Its conjugate base spreads the negative charge over a larger cage, so it is more stable.
3. Where does MeCl/AlCl3 substitute B5H9 at 25 °C, and at 200 °C?
The apex (1-Me, kinetic) at 25 °C; a basal boron (2-Me, thermodynamic) at 200 °C.
4. Show that B10H12(MeCN)2 should be arachno.
Each MeCN replaces H⁻ with a 2e donor: it is equivalent to B10H14²⁻ = B10H16 after adding 2 H⁺, i.e. BnHn+6.
Based on this deck.
Q4 uses the "add protons to neutralise" trick from Deck 3: B10H14 2− + 2H+ = B10H16, which has the arachno formula BnHn+6.
