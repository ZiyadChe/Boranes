1 · Definition

What is a boride?

  • A boride is a binary compound of boron with a more electropositive element, usually a metal: MxBy.
  • Several hundred binary metal borides are known, from M4B to MB66 and beyond. Most have been characterised by X-ray diffraction since the 1930s.
  • Formulas rarely follow simple valence rules (Ni3B, Ru11B8, CaB6, YB66). They are not salts of a "B3−" ion.
  • As the boron content rises, more B–B bonds form, with B–B ≈ 160–185 pm, as in the boranes.
classM : Bboron arrangement

Non-metal "borides"

Boron also forms solids with non-metals, such as boron carbide (B4C) and B12P2. They are built from B12 icosahedra like elemental boron.

Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3, Table 13.3.1; D. M. P. Mingos, 50th-anniversary review of electron counting (2021): > 200 binary borides, X-ray structures since 1932; Muetterties (1967), ch. 1 (multicentre bonding in borides).
Stress that borides are not ionic salts. The boron atoms bond to each other more and more as the B : M ratio rises, and the bonding in boron-rich borides is the multicentre bonding of the boranes.

All the routes need high temperatures, because the products are refractory. The choice depends on the product: bulk powders by methods 1, 3 and 4; coatings by CVD (method 5); single crystals by fused-salt electrolysis or from metal fluxes. Ask students to check that each equation balances.
Part B

Properties and uses

Hard, refractory, often metallic.

B
Properties and uses.
Properties

Properties follow from the boron framework

  • Refractory: TiB2, ZrB2 and HfB2 melt above ≈ 3000 °C, higher than the parent metals.
  • Hard and stiff: strong B–B and M–B bonding; B4C and TiB2 are among the hardest materials known.
  • Electrical: metal-rich and transition-metal MB2 borides are metallic; TiB2 conducts better than titanium itself. Boron-rich borides such as B4C are semiconductors.
  • Chemically resistant: most transition-metal borides resist non-oxidising acids; they are attacked by hot oxidising acids and alkaline oxidising melts.
  • Oxidation: in air at high temperature a B2O3 glaze forms and protects the surface until it evaporates (above ≈ 1100 °C).
  • Hydrolysis: borides of electropositive metals react with acid. Stock obtained the first boranes from "magnesium boride" + HCl.

Trend with boron content

More boron gives more B–B bonding, lower density, greater hardness and more covalent, less metallic behaviour.

Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3 (high m.p., hardness, wear resistance, chemical resistance); Wade (1971), §4-3 (hydrolysis of magnesium boride); m.p., conductivity and oxidation values: Greenwood & Earnshaw (1997), §6.3.2 (recalled, approximate).
Link each property to the structure: the framework of B–B bonds makes borides hard and refractory, and electrons not needed by the framework make many of them metallic. The hydrolysis point links back to Stock's work in Deck 1.

Did you know?

The strongest magnets are borides

  • Nd2Fe14B magnets were reported in 1984, independently by Sagawa (Sumitomo) and by Croat (General Motors).
  • They are the strongest permanent magnets in common use: in hard-disk drives, earphones, electric-vehicle motors and wind turbines.
  • Only about 1 % of the mass is boron. It is a metal-rich boride: each B atom is isolated in a trigonal prism of Fe atoms, the first class in the catenation table.

Metal borides

As boron content rises, boron links up

Metal-rich (M4B to M2B)

Isolated B atoms in metal cages; behave like interstitial compounds.

Intermediate (M3B2 to MB2)

Pairs, chains, double chains, then planar nets.

Boron-rich (MB4 and beyond)

3D networks of octahedra, cubo-octahedra or icosahedra; metals sit in cages.

Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3.1, Fig. 13.3.1; uses: high m.p., hardness, wear resistance, rocket cones and turbine blades (Li, p. 464).
Several hundred binary borides are known. Borides are hard, high-melting and chemically resistant, which is why they are used in rocket cones and turbine blades.

What to notice

B–B bonds are 160–185 pm throughout, close to those in the boranes. Only their number per atom changes: 0 (isolated), 1 (pairs), 2 (chains), 3 (nets) and 4–5 (polyhedra), ending in clusters of icosahedra.

Metal borides · MB2

Why the boron sheets look like graphite

  • Mg gives up two electrons: Mg → Mg2+ + 2 e−.
  • Each B then has 3 + 1 = 4 valence electrons, like C: [B2]2− ≡ C2, so the B sheets are graphite-like.
  • The transfer is incomplete in reality: holes left in the B–B σ bands make MgB2 a superconductor (Tc = 39 K, 2001).
Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3.1(1) (Tc 39 K); J. Nagamatsu et al., Nature 2001, 410, 63 (recalled citation); isoelectronic B−/C analogy: general textbook treatment.
The B− ≡ C analogy is the simplest way into MgB2. It is approximate.
Metal borides · MB6

CaB6: B6 octahedra linked in three dimensions

Ca in a cage of eight B6 octahedra (CsCl-like)
drag to rotate
  • Cubic: B6 octahedra at the cube corners, joined along all six axes by B–B bonds; Ca in the 24-coordinate cage (CsCl-like packing of Ca and B6).
  • Calculations show a net transfer of only 0.9–1.0 e, not 2: this is why metal-deficient M1−xB6 and even KB6 are stable.
Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3.1(3), Fig. 13.3.3; Wade (1971), §3 (6 + 14 electrons per B6); first MO treatment: Longuet-Higgins & Roberts, Proc. R. Soc. A 1954, 224, 336; model built from a = 414.5 pm, B at (x, ½, ½), x ≈ 0.20 (recalled parameters).
The count is Wade's rule again: a closo-B6 needs 7 skeletal pairs (14 electrons), and the six exo bonds now go to neighbouring octahedra instead of H atoms, as in B6H6 2−. Formally the framework is [B6]2−. LaB4 in panel (a) has chains of B6 octahedra joined by B2 pairs.
Metal borides · MB6 and MB4

Other hexaborides, and the tetraboride LaB4

  • M2+ (Ca, Sr, Ba, Eu): fill the framework's bonding levels; semiconductors or poor metals.
  • M3+ (La and early lanthanides): one electron more than the framework needs. It enters a conduction band, so LaB6 is a metal.
  • Fewer electrons: because the real transfer is ≈ 1 e, M1−xB6 and KB6 survive.
  • Small late lanthanides (Ho, Er, Tm, Lu) are too small to fill the 24-coordinate MB6 site; they form MB4 instead, with M–B 185–200 pm.

LaB4 (ThB4 type, tetragonal)

Chains of B6 octahedra run along c, linked sideways in the ab plane by B2 pairs into a 3D framework. The La atoms sit in tunnels along c.

Metal borides · boron-rich

Cubo-octahedron ⇌ icosahedron

twelve B atoms, 24 fixed edges (grey) + 6 square diagonals (violet)
drag to rotate
YB12NaB15

  • A cubo-octahedron has 8 triangular and 6 square faces; an icosahedron has 20 triangles.
  • Twist the 8 triangles in turn and pull them inward: each square folds along one diagonal into two triangles.
  • When the 6 diagonals (violet) become as short as the edges, the cage is an icosahedron: 24 + 6 = 30 edges.
  • In borides the shift is small: Li's arrows show the displacements that relate YB12 to NaB15.
Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3.1(4), Fig. 13.3.4; animation: vertices (0, ±a, ±b) and cyclic permutations with constant edge length, a/b from 1 (cubo-octahedron) to 1/φ (icosahedron), computed for this deck.

Summary

Key points

  • Borides MxBy range from M4B to MB66; their formulas do not follow simple valence rules.
  • They are made at high temperature from the elements, by reducing oxides with B, C/B4C or Mg/Al, by CVD, or by fused-salt electrolysis.
  • They are refractory, hard and often metallic: TiB2, ZrB2, LaB6, MgB2, FeB/Fe2B, Nd2Fe14B.
  • Rising B content: isolated B → B2 pairs → chains → double chains → nets (MB2) → B6 octahedra → B12 polyhedra → B12⊂(B12)12.
  • Electron counting explains MgB2 (B− ≡ C, graphite-like) and CaB6 (20 e per B6, only ≈ 1 e actually transferred).
Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3; Greenwood & Earnshaw (1997), §6.3.
Ask students to place an unknown boride, for example CrB or TiB2, in the catenation sequence from its formula.
Check your understanding

Questions (press → to reveal each answer)

1. Predict the boron arrangement in FeB and in TiB2.
FeB (MB): zigzag chains. TiB2 (MB2): graphite-like hexagonal nets (AlB2 type).
2. Give a route to a thin TiB2 coating.
CVD: TiCl4 + 2 BCl3 + 5 H2 → TiB2 + 10 HCl on a hot substrate.
3. Why does KB6 exist although B6 "needs" two electrons?
Only ≈ 1 e is actually transferred to each B6, even in CaB6; the framework tolerates fewer electrons.
4. Why is LaB6 used as an electron source?
Low work function (≈ 2.7 eV), metallic conduction, high m.p. and low volatility.
