The closo borane anions

The family BnHn²⁻, n = 6 to 12

B12H12²⁻ · icosahedron · Ih
drag to rotate
Mingos (2021), §5, Fig. 12, pp. 17–19; E. L. Muetterties, W. H. Knoth, Polyhedral Boranes, Dekker, 1968; models: idealised deltahedra (Deck 3).
All members have n + 1 skeletal pairs (4n + 2 valence electrons). They are all deltahedra, unlike the open neutral boranes. B8H8 2− is the dodecahedron; MO calculations had favoured a square antiprism.

  • Compare B5H9 (pyrophoric) and B2H6 (hydrolysed at once). The closed shell of n + 1 pairs and the absence of open faces and bridges remove the easy reaction paths.
  • Reactions go by substitution, keeping the cage, as aromatics keep the ring.

"Aromatic" boron hydrides

Hawthorne grouped the closo anions, the carboranes and their metal derivatives as the "aromatic" boron hydrides: stabilised by delocalisation, like benzene, but in three dimensions.

Hawthorne (1967), §5.6 intro and §5.6A.3a, pp. 286, 289–290.
The 810 °C figure is a good one for students to remember. Note the low toxicity: large doses of Na2B10H10 were given to humans without serious effect, which mattered for the later medical uses.
The closo borane anions

Reading structure from 11B NMR

  • B12H12²⁻: one doublet. All twelve borons equivalent (Ih); each couples to its one terminal H.
  • B10H10²⁻: two doublets, area 1 : 4 reading up-field: 2 apical (low field) and 8 equatorial.
  • The B10H10²⁻ structure was first deduced from this spectrum, then confirmed by the X-ray structure of Cu2B10H10.
  • Substitution breaks the symmetry, so NMR also locates substituents (apical or equatorial).
Hawthorne (1967), §5.6A.1–2, pp. 287, 289. Spectra drawn schematically (positions and line widths not to scale).
The 1:4 ratio with the smaller signal at lower field is characteristic. The schematic spectra show doublets from 11B–1H coupling.

  • B10H10²⁻ is far more reactive than B12H12²⁻. Relative H/D exchange rates: B12 1, B10 equatorial 68, B10 apical 330.
  • Apical borons of B10H10²⁻ carry most negative charge (Hoffmann and Lipscomb), and many electrophiles attack there first.
  • But the pattern is not universal: amination and benzoylation go equatorial. Hawthorne: "more than one type of electrophilic substitution reaction may exist".
Hawthorne (1967), §5.6A.3b–c, pp. 290–297; Das, §10.13.2(d) (reactivity apical B10 > equatorial B10 ≥ B12).
The perhalo ions B12Cl12 2− and B10Cl10 2− are even more inert than the parents. The ketone B10H9COPh 2− forms a semicarbazone and undergoes a Baeyer–Villiger oxidation, like an organic ketone.
Chemistry of the closo anions

Charge-compensated derivatives: neutral cages

B10H10²⁻ → HNO2 → red explosive intermediate → NaBH4 → 1,10-B10H8(N2)2

  • Each N2 (or CO, NR3, SMe2) is a neutral 2e donor that replaces H⁻ and cancels one negative charge.
  • B10H8(N2)2: a boron "bis-diazonium"; N2 is displaced by amines and nitriles with retention.
  • 1,10-B10H8(CO)2, m.p. 155 °C: the CO groups react like acyl groups (→ esters, amides, acids, pKa ≈ 9).

Electron count is unchanged

Replacing B–H⁻ by B–L keeps the skeletal count: L supplies the same two electrons to the exo bond as H⁻. So B10H8(N2)2 is still closo with 11 pairs, and neutral.

The same idea gives B10H12L2 (Deck 5) and the neutral carboranes below.

Hawthorne (1967), §5.6A.3b, pp. 294–295 (Knoth and co-workers, ref. 264).
The phrase "charge-compensated" is Hawthorne's. It is the bridge between anions and neutral heteroboranes: a carbon atom (CH) is a built-in charge compensation, as Part C shows.
Chemistry of the closo anions

Cages that rearrange and cages that join

Polyhedral rearrangement

  • 1,10-, 2,7- and 1,6-B10H8(NMe3)2 interconvert at 300–325 °C without breaking B–N bonds: the cage itself reorganises.
  • 2,3 → 1,6: first order, ΔH‡ ≈ 37 kcal mol⁻¹ (≈ 155 kJ mol⁻¹).
  • B12H10(NMe3)2: no clear rearrangement up to 500 °C.

Oxidative coupling

2 B10H10²⁻ + 4 Fe³⁺ → B20H18²⁻ + 2 H⁺ + 4 Fe²⁺

  • Two B10 cages joined by three-centre bonds.
  • Na/NH3 reduction gives B20H18⁴⁻: two B10H9²⁻ joined by a 2c B–B bond (e², ae, a² isomers; a² most stable).
  • B12H12²⁻ does not couple this way.
Hawthorne (1967), §5.6A.3d–e, pp. 297–300, Figs. 5.27–5.28.
The rearrangement mechanism proposed involves distortion of the cage through a more open intermediate. The B20H18 chemistry later became important in BNCT research.
Did you know?

Boron cages are used to fight cancer

  • Boron neutron capture therapy (BNCT): 10B (20 % of natural boron) captures a slow neutron and splits into an α particle and 7Li.
  • The fragments travel only about one cell diameter, so the damage stays where the boron is.
  • Clusters deliver many boron atoms per molecule. Na2B12H11SH ("BSH") has been used in clinical studies; the low toxicity of the closo ions made this possible. Hawthorne championed the field.

10B + nth
→ 4He²⁺ + 7Li³⁺

+ γ (≈ 2.3 MeV released in total)

M. F. Hawthorne, Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. Engl. 1993, 32, 950; Hawthorne (1967), p. 290 (toxicity); Greenwood & Earnshaw, 2nd edn, §6.1.
The 2.3 MeV figure is the commonly quoted energy release for the 10B(n,α)7Li reaction; check the current literature for clinical details before quoting specific trials.

Carboranes

The key relation: BH⁻ ≡ CH

  • C has one more electron than B: BH⁻ and CH are isoelectronic and isolobal (3 skeletal electrons).
  • BnHn²⁻ → CBn−1Hn⁻ → C2Bn−2Hn: same cage, same 4n + 2 electrons, now neutral.

Carboranes · history

A discovery kept secret

  • Mass spectra of borane–acetylene mixtures in a silent discharge hinted at carboranes in 1953.
  • The work was part of the US and Soviet high-energy fuel programmes and stayed classified into the early 1960s.
  • 1962: Williams, Onak and Shapiro publish C2B3H5, C2B4H6 and C2B5H7.
  • 1963: ten back-to-back papers in Inorganic Chemistry from Olin Mathieson and Thiokol describe C2B10H12; Soviet work (where o-carborane was called "barene") appears at the same time.

Who discovered them?

Mingos: unravelling who first made carboranes "is problematic because the work was not published in the open scientific literature". Hawthorne adds that a large body of Rohm and Haas work was never published.

Mingos (2021), p. 20 and refs. 106–117; Hawthorne (1967), §5.6B.1–2 and 6a–b, pp. 301–305.
This links to the zip-fuel story of Deck 3. Cold War secrecy delayed the field by about a decade.

Carbon prefers low-connectivity vertices and positions far apart from each other.

Carbon is more electronegative and contributes more electrons; it occupies vertices with fewer neighbours, and two carbons are most stable when separated. This explains both the 1,2 → 1,6 isomerisation here and the icosahedral isomers on the next slide.
Carboranes

The icosahedral carboranes: ortho, meta, para

1,2 (450–500 °C) → 1,7 (500–620 °C) → 1,12

  • Names: ortho (1,2, "barene"), meta (1,7, "neocarborane"), para (1,12).
  • Para-carborane: 11B NMR one doublet, all ten B equivalent.
  • MO calculations: 1,7 and 1,12 comparable, both more stable than 1,2. The C atoms carry net positive charge.
Hawthorne (1967), §5.6B.6a, pp. 302–305; ortho-carborane synthesis: Das, §10.13.2(d)(iii) and §10.12.8.
The synthesis is the B10H12L2 route of Deck 5: the acetylene supplies two CH vertices, closing the arachno B10 cage to an icosahedron.
Carboranes · mechanism

How can carbons move across an icosahedron?

  • Lipscomb: stretch six edges so that the icosahedron opens to a cuboctahedron, then close the square faces the other way.
  • This one step converts 1,2 into 1,7 without breaking any bond to a substituent.

A puzzle left open

This path cannot give the 1,12 isomer, yet 1,7 → 1,12 is observed at higher temperature. Other pathways, such as rotation of a triangular face, must also operate.

Hawthorne (1967), §5.6B.6a, p. 305 (Lipscomb and co-workers, ref. 276), Fig. 5.30.
The figure labels four atoms A, B, C, D to follow them through the process. This "diamond–square–diamond" type of process was later developed by Lipscomb and others for many cluster rearrangements.
Carboranes · chemistry

The carboranyl group: bulky and electron-withdrawing

  • C–H is weakly acidic: RLi gives LiC2B10H10Li, which reacts like an organolithium (CO2, aldehydes, R3SiCl …).
  • Substituents on C behave as if attached to a strongly electron-withdrawing group with large steric demand and great kinetic stability.
  • Direct chlorination gives B-perchloro carboranes of exceptional thermal stability; carborane–siloxane polymers were developed for heat-resistant materials.

Removing one boron: the dicarbollide ion

→ 7,8-C2B9H12⁻ + H2 + B(OMe)3

C2B9H12⁻ + NaH
→ [C2B9H11]²⁻ + H2

  • Base attacks the most positive boron, B3/B6, beside both carbons.
  • Wade count: 6 + 18 + 2 = 26 e, 13 pairs, n = 11 → nido icosahedron.

The bridge to Deck 8

Hawthorne saw that [C2B9H11]²⁻ should bind metals like cyclopentadienide: the origin of the metallacarboranes, beginning with the "ferrocene analogue" [Fe(C2B9H11)2]²⁻.

Hawthorne (1967), §5.6B.7, pp. 308–310, Figs. 5.31–5.32; Mingos (2021), pp. 20–22.
"Dicarbollide" comes from the Spanish olla, a pot, describing the bowl-shaped ligand. The name was coined by Hawthorne.
Carboranes · applications

Modern uses of boron clusters

Weakly coordinating anions

CB11H12⁻ (isoelectronic with B12H12²⁻) and its halogenated forms are bulky, inert and very weakly basic: they stabilise highly reactive cations.

Carborane superacids

H(CHB11Cl11) (Reed, 2004) is far stronger than H2SO4, yet "gentle": its conjugate base does not attack, so the acid can be kept in glass.

Medicine

Boron-rich clusters for BNCT; the carboranyl group as a hydrophobic, three-dimensional replacement for phenyl in drug design.

Batteries

Na2B12H12 becomes a Na⁺ superionic conductor (≈ 0.1 S cm⁻¹) above an order–disorder transition near 529 K.

M. Juhasz, S. Hoffmann, E. Stoyanov, K.-C. Kim, C. A. Reed, Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 2004, 43, 5352; T. J. Udovic et al., Chem. Commun. 2014, 50, 3750; Hawthorne, Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. Engl. 1993, 32, 950.
All four uses trace back to the same two properties: the cage is very stable and the negative charge is spread over a large surface.
Did you know?

The strongest acid you can keep in a bottle

  • Reed's carborane acid H(CHB11Cl11) was reported as at least a million times stronger than concentrated sulfuric acid.
  • Yet it is not corrosive in the usual sense. Corrosion by HF comes from F⁻ attacking glass; the CHB11Cl11⁻ cage is too stable to attack anything.
  • The cage behind it is the same icosahedron that Longuet-Higgins predicted to be a closed shell in 1955.

Summary

Key points

  • Closo BnHn²⁻ (n = 6–12) are deltahedra with n + 1 pairs; Cs2B12H12 survives 810 °C; the hydronium salts are strong acids.
  • They react by substitution; B10H10²⁻ (apical especially) is more reactive than B12H12²⁻. Neutral donors give charge-compensated cages.
  • BH⁻ ≡ CH: C2Bn−2Hn are neutral closo carboranes; carbons prefer low-connectivity, well-separated sites.
  • o-Carborane from B10H12L2 + alkyne; 1,2 → 1,7 → 1,12 on heating; carboranyl is electron-withdrawing (pKa 2.87 vs 5.46).
  • Base removes B3 to give nido-C2B9H12⁻ and the Cp-like dicarbollide [C2B9H11]²⁻, the entry to Deck 8.
Hawthorne (1967), §5.6; Mingos (2021), §5; Das, §10.12.8.

Check your understanding

Questions (press → to reveal each answer)

1. Predict the 11B NMR pattern of para-carborane.
One doublet: all ten borons equivalent (D5d), each with one terminal H.
2. Why is 1,6-C2B4H6 more stable than 1,2?
The positively polarised C atoms are farther apart in the trans (1,6) arrangement.
3. Which B is removed when o-carborane meets methoxide, and why?
B3 (or B6), bonded to both carbons and therefore the most positive boron, so the most open to nucleophilic attack.
4. Show that [C2B9H11]²⁻ resembles C5H5⁻.
Its open C2B3 pentagonal face has five inward orbitals holding six electrons, like the 6π electrons of the Cp⁻ ring.
