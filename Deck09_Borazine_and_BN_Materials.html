Part A

B–N and C–C

Same number of electrons, different distribution.

A
Part A.
B–N and C–C

Isoelectronic, but polar

  • B (3 e) + N (5 e) = C (4 e) + C (4 e): a B–N unit is isoelectronic with C–C.
  • But N is much more electronegative than B (Pauling 3.04 vs 2.04). The σ bond is polarised Bδ+–Nδ−.
  • N's lone pair can be donated into B's empty p orbital: a dative π bond N→B, which pushes electron density the other way.
  • Result: "olefin-like" aminoboranes R2B=NR2 are only qualitatively like alkenes. They are not rigidly planar (B–N rotation barrier ≈ 20 kcal mol⁻¹, far below C=C) and often dimerise.
Muetterties (1967), ch. 1, pp. 19–20; Wade (1971), §4-4, p. 86; electronegativities: Pauling scale.
The whole deck is about this tension: same electron count as carbon, very different polarity. The π donation partly offsets the σ polarity, but not completely.
B–N and C–C

From H3N·BH3 to H2N=BH2 to rings

  • Ammonia borane H3N·BH3: a σ-donor adduct, isoelectronic with ethane (Deck 5).
  • Aminoborane H2N–BH2: B and N both three-coordinate, with N→B π donation; isoelectronic with ethene. The parent exists only as oligomers and polymers.
  • Substitution decides the outcome: (H2NBH2)x gives monomers, dimers (four-membered rings) and trimers (chair six-membered rings).
  • With two amino groups on boron, e.g. (Me2N)2BH, enough π donation reaches boron that dimerisation is unfavourable; B(NMe2)3 is planar.

A trimer that is not borazine

(H2N–BH2)3, cyclotriborazane, is a saturated chair like cyclohexane: four-coordinate B and N, no π system. Borazine is the same ring with 6 fewer H atoms, planar and π-bonded.

B3H6N3Me6 was shown by X-ray (Trefonas & Lipscomb, 1959) to have this chair form.

Hawthorne, in Muetterties (1967), §5.5A, pp. 245–246; Sheldon & Smith (1960), pp. 200–201, 208; Wade (1971), Fig. 4-11.
Cyclotriborazane B3N3H12 is to borazine B3N3H6 as cyclohexane is to benzene.

B
Part B.
Borazine · history

Discovery and name

  • Stock's vacuum line made the work possible, but early yields were only "a few millilitres of vapour".
  • The name: Wiberg's "Borazol" became borazole; an ACS committee recommended borazine, which Sheldon and Smith in 1960 still declined to use.

Same ring, more early chemistry

As early as 1889 Rideal made "BClNPh" from BCl3 and aniline. Jones and Kinney (1939) showed it was a trimer, B-trichloro-N-triphenylborazine, and related it to borazole.

Sheldon & Smith (1960), pp. 200–203, refs. 1–5, 25–26: A. Stock, E. Pohland, Ber. 1926, 59, 2215; A. Stock, R. Wierl, Z. anorg. Chem. 1931, 203, 228; E. Wiberg, A. Bolz, Ber. 1940, 73, 209.
A short history, tying back to Stock in Deck 1. Stock and Pohland obtained borazine by heating the diborane–ammonia adduct.

Borazine · bonding

The π system: shared, but unevenly

  • Both rings have six π electrons in three bonding π MOs.
  • In benzene the six p orbitals are identical: the MOs are spread evenly.
  • In borazine the N 2p orbitals lie lower in energy than the B 2p. The filled π MOs are mainly on N; the empty ones mainly on B.
  • So the π electrons are partly localised as N lone pairs: delocalisation is real but weaker than in benzene.

Evidence

Planarity, equal ring bonds, benzene-like UV bands and a measurable diamagnetic anisotropy (for B3Cl3N3H3) all point to some aromatic character; the chemistry points to a polar, much less aromatic ring.

Muetterties (1967), p. 20; Sheldon & Smith (1960), pp. 215–217 (Roothaan & Mulliken MO treatment); Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3. Energy diagram schematic.
This is the key bonding idea: an electronegativity difference splits the degenerate p orbital set and polarises the π MOs. Modern calculations generally describe borazine as weakly aromatic, far less than benzene.
Borazine · structures

Writing borazine: three ways

σ only: N keeps its lone pairs; B has empty p orbitals. No formal charges.

Kekulé-type: three dative N=B π bonds, with formal N+ and B−. Two such forms.

Circle: delocalised π, like benzene. Convenient, but hides the polarity.

Sheldon & Smith (1960), p. 200 (Kekulé structures accepted); Muetterties (1967), p. 20.
The formal charges in the Kekulé form (B−, N+) are opposite to the actual partial charges (B δ+, N δ−). This is a frequent source of confusion: formal charges are bookkeeping, not real charge distribution.

The general idea

3 BX3 + 3 NR3 → B3X3N3R3 + 6 RX (X = H, Cl, Br, alkyl; R = H, alkyl): a condensation at about 200 °C with loss of H2 or HX.

Sheldon & Smith (1960), pp. 201–206 (Stock & Pohland; Schaeffer & Anderson 1949; Jones & Kinney 1939; Brown & Laubengayer 1955).
Using NH4Cl rather than free NH3 moderates the reaction: free ammonia gives only traces of borazine with BCl3. A widely used modern laboratory route heats (NH4)2SO4 with NaBH4 in tetraglyme (not in the 1960 review).
Making borazines · mechanism

From ammonia borane to boron nitride, by losing H2

  • Wiberg's scheme: adduct → aminoborane → iminoborane → trimer. The free iminoborane HB≡NH is not isolable; Sheldon and Smith suggest cyclisation occurs at an earlier stage, through the saturated trimer.
  • The same sequence explains why NH3 + B2H6 gives borazine at about 200 °C and boron nitride at about 400 °C.
Sheldon & Smith (1960), pp. 207–209 (Wiberg's condensation chain); Wade (1971), §4-4, Fig. 4-11.
This is also the chemistry of ammonia borane as a hydrogen store: each step releases H2, and the end product is boron nitride. Regenerating ammonia borane from the spent material is the hard part.
Part D

Reactions of borazine

A polar ring that adds where benzene substitutes.

D
Part D.
Reactions

Addition, not substitution

  • HCl, HBr, H2O, MeOH and MeI add three molecules across the ring, even with excess borazine: B3N3H6·3HX.
  • Regiochemistry follows polarity: the nucleophile goes to B (δ+), H to N (δ−).
  • The adduct is a saturated chair ring, like B3H6N3Me6.
  • On heating it loses 3 H2 to give the B-substituted borazine, e.g. B3Cl3N3H3: overall substitution by addition–elimination.

Why all three?

Adding the first HX destroys much of the ring's π stabilisation; adding the next two then costs little, so 1 : 1 and 1 : 2 adducts are rare.

Sheldon & Smith (1960), pp. 206, 210 (adducts B3H3N3H3·3AD; structure VII).
Benzene does not add HCl at all under these conditions. The contrast is the clearest evidence that borazine's aromatic stabilisation is much smaller than benzene's.

  • Many look like ordinary nucleophilic substitution (B–Cl + EtO⁻ → B–OEt + Cl⁻).
  • Electrophilic substitution at N was "expected" in 1960 but no example was then known.

Sheldon & Smith (1960), pp. 205–207, 211–212, 216.
The B-Cl compound is the borazine analogue of an acid chloride: easily made, easily converted to other B-substituted borazines.
Reactions

Heating borazine: towards polymers and BN

  • Gaseous borazine: almost unchanged after 30 min at 200 °C; at 500 °C, 27 % decomposes in the same time to H2 and (BNH)x.
  • The liquid is much less stable, even at room temperature: decomposition is a condensation between molecules.
  • Controlled pyrolysis gives B6N6H10 and B5N5H8, analogues of biphenyl and naphthalene.
  • Cross-linked B-amino-borazine polymers were stable to 600 °C: early steps towards BN ceramics.

Polyborazylene

Linking borazine rings through B–N bonds gives polyborazylene, (B3N3H~4)x, a soluble precursor that converts on further heating to boron nitride fibres and coatings. Here molecular chemistry passes into materials chemistry.

Sheldon & Smith (1960), pp. 205, 209; polyborazylene: Greenwood & Earnshaw, 2nd edn, §6.3.
Note the parallel with organic chemistry: biphenyl and naphthalene analogues formed by losing H2 between rings.

Graphite stacks ABAB with carbon atoms over ring centres; h-BN stacks with B over N, an arrangement favoured by the B δ+ / N δ− charges. Graphite is black and conducting; h-BN is white and insulating because the B–N polarity opens a large band gap.
Boron nitride

Why h-BN is white and graphite is black

  • In graphite the π and π* bands touch: electrons are mobile and all visible light is absorbed.
  • In h-BN the B/N difference splits them. The filled π band lies mainly on N, the empty π* band on B (the same splitting as in borazine).
  • The gap is about 6 eV (≈ 210 nm): visible light passes, so the solid is white and insulating.
  • Watanabe, Taniguchi and Kanda (2004) measured a direct gap of 5.97 eV in pure crystals and obtained deep-ultraviolet laser emission at 215 nm.
Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3; K. Watanabe, T. Taniguchi, H. Kanda, Nature Mater. 2004, 3, 404 (doi:10.1038/nmat1134). Band diagram schematic.
Later work suggests the bulk h-BN gap is indirect, at a similar energy; the 2004 paper interpreted it as direct. Either way, the gap is far in the ultraviolet.
Boron nitride

Cubic BN (borazon): made under pressure

  • R. H. Wentorf (General Electric) made c-BN in 1957, shortly after GE's synthetic diamond.
  • h-BN → c-BN at ≈ 2000 K and > 50 kbar (≈ 5–7 GPa), with Li3N or Mg3N2 as catalyst/flux.
  • Zinc-blende structure: every B bonded tetrahedrally to four N, and vice versa, as C in diamond.
  • Second only to diamond in hardness among common materials; "Borazon" became GE's trademark.

Diamond's weakness

Diamond cannot be used to machine steel: at cutting temperatures carbon dissolves in iron. c-BN does not react with iron and resists oxidation to higher temperatures, so it is the standard super-abrasive for hardened steels.

Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3 (conditions); R. H. Wentorf, J. Chem. Phys. 1957, 26, 956; Borazon, Wikipedia (accessed Oct 2026).
The steel-machining advantage is the main industrial reason c-BN matters.
Did you know?

"White graphite" in cosmetics and in graphene devices

  • Hexagonal BN is soft, slippery, white and chemically inert. Fine BN powder is used in cosmetics for a smooth, matte finish.
  • Its flat, insulating layers are an almost perfect support for graphene: the lattice mismatch is only about 2 %, and there are no dangling bonds to scatter electrons.
  • Boron nitride nanotubes, the BN version of carbon nanotubes, were predicted in 1994 and made in 1995. Unlike carbon tubes, all are insulating whatever their structure.

BN inside organic molecules

Azaborines: one B–N in place of one C=C

  • Replacing one C=C unit of an aromatic hydrocarbon with B–N gives an azaborine. BN analogues of phenanthrene and naphthalene were made from the late 1950s.
  • Muetterties noted that their B–N and B–X bonds are far less reactive than in borazines, comparable to aromatic C–N and C–X: delocalisation is more extensive than in borazine.
  • The parent 1,2-dihydro-1,2-azaborine, C4BNH6, was isolated by Liu and co-workers (2009) and is aromatic.
  • BN/CC isosterism is now used in materials and drug design.
Muetterties (1967), ch. 1, pp. 20–21; Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3 (azaborane analogues); A. J. V. Marwitz, M. H. Matus, L. N. Zakharov, D. A. Dixon, S.-Y. Liu, Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 2009, 48, 973 (recalled citation).
One BN unit in a carbon ring is diluted by the surrounding carbons, so the polarity is less disruptive than in borazine, where every bond is B–N.
Summary

Key points

  • B–N is isoelectronic with C–C but polar (Bδ+Nδ−); N→B π donation fills boron's empty orbital.
  • Borazine: planar D3h ring, B–N 1.44 Å, b.p. 53 °C, six π electrons concentrated on N.
  • Made from BCl3 + NH4Cl (then LiBH4), from LiBH4 + RNH3Cl, or by dehydrogenating H3N·BH3.
  • It adds 3 HX (X to B, H to N); substitution happens at B; pyrolysis leads to polyborazylene and BN.
  • h-BN ≈ white, insulating graphite (gap ≈ 6 eV); c-BN ≈ diamond (Wentorf 1957), used on steel; azaborines extend BN/CC isosterism.
Sheldon & Smith (1960); Li, Zhou, Mak (2008), §13.3; Muetterties (1967), ch. 1.

Questions (press → to reveal each answer)

1. Give the product of borazine with 3 MeOH.
B3N3H6·3MeOH: a saturated chair ring with OMe added to each B and H to each N. On heating it loses 3 H2 to give B-trimethoxyborazine.
2. Why is h-BN white but graphite black?
B–N polarity splits the π and π* bands, giving a ≈ 6 eV gap; graphite's bands touch.
3. Why does borazine add HCl when benzene does not?
Its π stabilisation is small and its B–N bonds are polar: Cl⁻ attacks electrophilic B and H⁺ goes to N, and little aromatic energy is lost.
4. Why is c-BN, not diamond, used to machine steel?
Diamond's carbon dissolves in hot iron; c-BN does not react with iron and resists oxidation.
